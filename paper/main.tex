	\caption{\textbf{Evidence integration in four sequential estimation tasks.} In each task, participants view a sequence of inputs and report a real-valued estimate after each observation. Tasks differ in input type, sequence length, and repetition structure. (\textbf{A}) The \textit{Value Integration Task} \cite{yoo2025people} presents pairs of snack items and asks participants to report the mean value difference between the accumulated snacks on the left vs right. Each trial includes 30 observations, and snack sequences are never repeated. (\textbf{B}) The \textit{Proportion Inference Task} \cite{prat2024imprecise} presents rings of two colors drawn randomly from a box and asks participants to report the hidden proportion of one color. Each trial includes 5 observations, leading to frequent repetition of color sub-sequences. (\textbf{C, D}) The \textit{Binary} and \textit{Continuous Integration Tasks} (both new) present binary (continuous) draws from a hidden distribution and ask participants to report a running estimate of the proportion (mean) of the sequence so far. Each trial includes 15 observations, and the first few are drawn from a small pool of fixed sub-sequences, producing partial repetition across trials.}
	\label{fig:tasks_overview}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure} % Do NOT use \begin{figure*} -- single-column template
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/models_overview_paper.pdf}
	\caption{\textbf{A dynamic learning-rate model and its spiking neural network implementation.} (\textbf{A}) An error-driven update rule integrates each observation $I_n$ into a running estimate $V_n$ with a learning rate that decays as a power law of the observation count $n$. The discounting exponent $\lambda$ sets the resulting integration strategy (see Supplementary Text): $\lambda=1$ recovers the unweighted running mean, $\lambda<1$ slows the decay and over-weights recent observations (recency), and $\lambda>1$ steepens it and over-weights early observations (primacy). (\textbf{B}) A spiking neural network implements the same computation using three functionally- and anatomically-distinct neural populations. Each observation $I_n$ feeds directly into \textit{error} and into \textit{count}, which recurrently tracks the observation number and decodes the discounting weight that modulates \textit{error}. \textit{Error} computes the discounted prediction error between the observation and the current value estimate, and drives \textit{value}, which maintains a working memory of $V_n$ through recurrent excitation and feeds it back to \textit{error} for the next comparison. (\textbf{C}) Spiking activity (rasters) and decoded traces from one example trial (four observations, shaded inter-trial intervals). \textit{Count} decodes $\alpha(n)$ and $n$; \textit{error} tracks the discounted prediction error, rising at each observation and decaying between them; and \textit{value} integrates and holds its estimate.}
	\label{fig:models_overview}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure} % Do NOT use \begin{figure*} -- single-column template
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/model_performance.pdf}
	\caption{\textbf{RL-$\lambda$ outperforms classic integration models on most tasks while the SNN implementation remains competitive despite spiking noise.} Each model was fit separately to every participant via cross-validated RMSE between predicted and human responses; boxplots show the resulting per-participant distribution (one point per participant). Mean, LeakyIntegrator, and PrimacyRecency are widely used baselines for evidence-integration behavior: Mean is the optimal unweighted running average \cite{degroot1974reaching}; LeakyIntegrator applies the same update rule as RL-$\lambda$ but with a constant, rather than power-law-decaying, learning rate \cite{usher2001time, glaze2015normative}; PrimacyRecency up-weighs earlier and more recent observations via two free decay parameters \cite{pooley2011understanding, galdo2022quest}. RL-$\lambda$ has significantly lower RMSE than all three baselines on the Value Integration (\textbf{A}) and Proportion Inference (\textbf{B}) tasks (stars: paired Wilcoxon vs.\ RL-$\lambda$), and than Mean and LeakyIntegrator on Binary (\textbf{C}) and Continuous (\textbf{D}) Integration; PrimacyRecency ties RL-$\lambda$ on Binary Integration and significantly outperforms it on Continuous Integration. SNN, which reproduces RL-$\lambda$'s computation within a biologically plausible network, necessarily has higher RMSE than RL-$\lambda$ due to spike noise and real-time dynamics, but remains comparable to the classic baselines. Model comparisons were consistent when validated using a second fit metric, negative log-likelihood (see Supplementary Text).}
	\label{fig:model_performance}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure} % Do NOT use \begin{figure*} -- single-column template
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/lambda_main_edited.pdf}
	\caption{\textbf{Magnitude of response change decays with evidence accumulated, and individuals differ systematically in how quickly.} (\textbf{A--C}) Human response change (gray) is the median, across participants, of each participant's trial-averaged $|\Delta R|$ -- the absolute change between one response and the next -- at each point in the sequence. Response change shrinks as people gather more evidence in three of the four tasks (see Supplementary Text for a discussion of the exception). (\textbf{D--F}) Fitting a power-law curve to each participant's response-change curve reveals large individual differences in temporal discounting $\lambda$. Consistent with previous work \cite{yoo2025people}, most individuals show a moderate-to-strong recency bias in most tasks ($\lambda<1$), but a substantial minority show near-optimal discounting or even a primacy bias ($\lambda\geq1$). The distribution of $\lambda$ differs across tasks, but individuals show significant consistency in $\lambda$ both within and across tasks (see Supplementary Text). PrimacyRecency, RL-$\lambda$, and SNN all correctly predict this decay, but LeakyIntegrator's response-change curve is nearly constant, and Mean cannot capture individual differences (see Supplementary Text).}
	\label{fig:lambda_main}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure} % Do NOT use \begin{figure*} -- single-column template
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/sigma_main.pdf}
	\caption{\textbf{Only the spiking network reproduces the dynamics of human response variability.} (\textbf{A--C}) Even when a trial's opening sequence is exactly repeated, human responses vary from trial to trial. We quantify this as $\sigma$, the standard deviation of a participant's responses across trials with an identical input sequence (adapted from \cite{prat2024imprecise}). $\sigma$ differs systematically across individuals on three of four tasks (Value Integration is excluded: its inputs are never repeated), with significant within- and across-task consistency (see Supplementary Text). To investigate the underlying mechanisms, we fit noise-augmented variants of the mathematical models and compared them to SNN and human data. (\textbf{D--F}) Response noise grows over the course of a repeated sequence for humans and SNN, but not for any response-noise model. (\textbf{G--I}) Trial-wise deviations from a participant's typical response are strongly autocorrelated across observations for humans and SNN, but not for the response-noise models. These two dynamic signatures reflect the noise inherent in SNN's internal representations, which arise from spike noise and propagate forward in time, unlike the models' independent per-response noise draws.}
	\label{fig:sigma_main}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure} % Do NOT use \begin{figure*} -- single-column template
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/neural_main.pdf}
	\caption{\textbf{SNN dynamics link neural activity to behavior, yielding concrete, testable predictions.} Columns show, left to right: network dynamics for representative parameter values; how neural and behavioral measures of those dynamics scale with a key model parameter; and how the two measures covary directly. (\textbf{A--C}) After a run of consistent observations, one outlier evokes a sharp rise in decoded prediction-error (PE) activity that decays before the next inter-trial interval; both the peak response and its decay rate scale with the learning rate $\alpha_0$. We predict that the peak PE response to a surprising observation will predict how quickly that response then decays. (\textbf{D--F}) Activity of error-sensitive neurons decays over a trial at a rate set by the discounting parameter $\lambda$. We predict that this neural decay rate will track an individual's own behavioral decay in response-change magnitude. (\textbf{G--I}) Spiking noise causes the decoded value estimate to drift across repeated presentations of an identical sequence, and this noise shrinks as more neurons are engaged. We predict that a neural measure of this trial-to-trial variability (such as coefficient of variation) will correlate with an individual's own behavioral response variance $\sigma$. All results are from simulations in the Continuous Integration Task.}
	\label{fig:neural_main}
\end{figure}
