\begin{abstract} \bfseries \boldmath
To navigate the world, the brain must update its estimates as new evidence arrives from uncertain, dynamic environments, yet the neurocognitive mechanisms underlying evidence integration remain unclear. We analyzed human behavior across four evidence integration tasks (two established, two newly developed), and developed a biologically-plausible spiking neural network that tracks evidence using discounted prediction error. This network reproduced human behavior, captured individual differences that are reliable within and across tasks, and linked neural mechanisms, like synaptic learning and error modulation, to behavioral differences, such as outlier sensitivity and recency bias. The network uniquely reproduced the growth and persistence of response variability within a repeated sequence and causally dissociated two candidate working-memory mechanisms through transient disruption. Together, these results yield four falsifiable predictions that link neural activity to behavior and provide a generative, extensible account of human evidence integration that generalizes across tasks and individuals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Successful decision making requires individuals to make predictions about the outcomes of their choices and continuously update their estimates by integrating new evidence and feedback from the environment. These tasks are especially challenging in naturalistic environments, which are inherently dynamic and stochastic, requiring different strategies for integrating observations, choices, and outcomes over time. Despite the ubiquity of evidence integration in natural and experimental settings, the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying individual differences in integration strategies remain unclear. Models that update an internal estimate using prediction error -- the weighted discrepancy between an expectation and each new observation -- have successfully captured behavior across a range of tasks and species \cite{sutton2018reinforcement, palminteri2022computational, palminteri2017confirmation, camerer1999experience, godara2025apparent}, including in tasks that require integrating a stream of sequential, noisy evidence into a running estimate \cite{behrens2007learning, nassar2010approximately, yu2008sequential, prat2024imprecise}. However, many of these models fail to account for individual variability \cite{cain2012computational, gibson1997learning, waskom2018decision, kopec2024integrate}, are descriptive rather than mechanistic \cite{sackett2017individual, ackerman1987individual}, and stop short of showing that the required computations can be carried out in neural networks \cite{averbeck2022reinforcement, oconnell2018bridging}. This leaves open questions about how the proposed computational mechanisms generalize across behavioral tasks and map onto neural mechanisms in the brain. Characterizing the cognitive processes underlying individual variability, and identifying the corresponding neural mechanisms, can significantly improve our understanding of learning and decision making \cite{goodhew2019translating, boogert2018measuring}.

In this study, we investigated four evidence integration tasks, two from prior work and two tasks developed for this study, and characterized individual differences in cognitive strategies within and across tasks. We then proposed the Discounted Prediction Error (DPE) model, implemented it in a biologically-plausible spiking neural network (SNN), and identified the neural mechanisms underlying behavioral signatures seen in the human data. DPE successfully recreated the behavior of individual participants across all four tasks, generally matched or outperformed alternative models from the literature, and captured significant individual differences in cognitive strategy. The SNN reproduced these behavioral results, was the only model capable of recreating the observed response variability dynamics, dissociated two candidate neural mechanisms for working memory via a simulated causal perturbation, and generated four falsifiable predictions linking neural activity to behavior. Together, these results suggest that a simple, error-driven mechanism, implemented in spiking neurons, offers a plausible and generative account of both the shared and idiosyncratic ways people integrate evidence over time.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Cognitive tasks}
To investigate different aspects of evidence integration and test the generalizability of our model, we studied four evidence-integration tasks (Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks_overview}). Each task presents a sequence of noisy observations and asks for a real-valued estimate after every observation, requiring participants to integrate all evidence observed so far in the trial. The four tasks collectively span multiple dimensions of task design, including input type, sequence length, repetition structure, and estimated quantity. Two of these tasks reanalyze previously published data \cite{prat2024imprecise, yoo2025people}, while two were conducted as part of the current study. Task procedures, stimuli, and participants are detailed in Materials and Methods.

\subsection{Discounted prediction error model}
In the Discounted Prediction Error model (DPE, Fig.~\ref{fig:models_overview}A), the learner maintains a running estimate $V_n$, computes a prediction error between its current estimate and each new observation $I_n$, and updates its estimate according to:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dpe_update}
    V_n = V_{n-1} + \alpha(n) \bigl(I_n - V_{n-1}\bigr)
\end{equation}

\noindent The size of this update depends on the dynamic learning rate $\alpha(n)$, which in turn depends on a baseline learning rate $\alpha_0$, the number of observations $n$ already accumulated, and a discounting rate $\lambda$:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dpe_alpha}
    \alpha(n) = \frac{\alpha_0}{n^\lambda}
\end{equation}

This single mechanism can reproduce a range of biases documented in the sequential-integration literature. When $\lambda=0$, Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_alpha} recovers a constant learning rate $\alpha_0$, equivalent to a leaky integrator with fixed update size \cite{usher2001time, glaze2015normative}; when $\lambda=1$, it recovers the unweighted running mean, the statistically optimal estimator under stationary generative statistics. More generally, $\lambda<1$ slows the learning-rate decay and over-weights recent observations (a recency bias), while $\lambda>1$ steepens it and over-weights early observations (a primacy bias); see Supplementary Text for a mathematical proof. Unlike descriptive models that assign each observation its weight via a post-hoc process (e.g., a weight curve fit directly to the data), DPE generates a range of weighting patterns from a single generative process -- one which runs in real time and does not require a complete memory of past observations. Although other models that dynamically weight prediction error have successfully explained probabilistic reasoning and reward learning across a range of tasks and volatility regimes \cite{behrens2007learning, piray2021model, nassar2010approximately, yu2008sequential, camerer1999experience}, they have not yet been applied to the heterogeneous suite of evidence-integration tasks studied here, used to characterize systematic individual differences in integration strategy, or (most importantly) implemented in a biologically plausible network.

We implemented the DPE model as a spiking neural network (SNN) using the Neural Engineering Framework \cite{eliasmith2003neural}, a method for constructing biologically plausible networks of spiking neurons that realize arbitrary computations (Fig.~\ref{fig:models_overview}B). Three interacting neural populations implement Eqs.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}--\ref{eq:dpe_alpha}, leading to the expected DPE dynamics (Fig.~\ref{fig:models_overview}C): an \textit{error} population computes the prediction error $E_n$ between each new observation $I_n$ and the previous estimate $V_{n-1}$, discounted by the weight $\alpha(n)$; a \textit{value} population maintains the current estimate $V_n$ in working memory through recurrent excitation, updating in response to \textit{error}'s drive; and a \textit{counting} network tracks the observation index $n$ and decodes $\alpha(n)$ to modulate \textit{error}. Because $\alpha(n)$ emerges from this counting circuit's spiking dynamics, the resulting discounting schedule (and subsequent biases) arises directly from the network's biophysics. Full architectural and simulation details are provided in Materials and Methods.

\subsection{Model performance}
We assessed how well each model accounts for human behavior by fitting its free parameters separately to every participant and computing the root-mean-squared error (RMSE) between each model's predicted responses and the participant's actual responses. We compared DPE and its SNN implementation against three baseline models spanning a range of integration strategies: Mean, which computes the optimal running average with no free parameters \cite{degroot1974reaching}; LeakyIntegrator (LI), which uses the same error-driven update but with a constant learning rate \cite{usher2001time, glaze2015normative}; and PrimacyRecency (PR), which up-weights early and late observations via two independently fit decay parameters \cite{pooley2011understanding, galdo2022quest, yoo2025people}. Model equations and fitting procedures are detailed in Materials and Methods, and were unchanged across tasks; only free parameters were allowed to vary.

DPE achieved the best, or among the best, fit to human behavior on every task (Fig.~\ref{fig:model_performance}). It had significantly lower RMSE than all three baseline models on both Proportion Inference and Value Integration, where it was also the single best-fitting model for the large majority of participants (86\% and 76\%, respectively). On Binary and Continuous Integration, DPE significantly outperformed Mean and LI, but PR matched it on the former and significantly outperformed it on the latter, making PR the modal best-fitting individual model on both (67\% and 70\%, respectively; see Table~\ref{tab:best_fit}). However, these per-participant win counts overstate the size of this gap: the median RMSE difference between DPE and PR was negligible on Binary Integration (0.4\%, not significant, $p$=0.40) and modest even where reliable on Continuous Integration (2.4\% lower for PR, $p$=0.004), with negative log-likelihood showing the same pattern (see Supplementary Text). Table~\ref{tab:effect_sizes} places this in context: DPE's median margin over the other models across all four tasks is 14.2\% (range 2.3--74.8\%), substantially larger than the 0.4\% and 2.4\% by which PR edges it out. The SNN fit worst of all five models on Continuous Integration and worse than two of the three baselines on Binary Integration, but remained close to the baselines on Proportion Inference (+5.6\%) and Value Integration (+4.9\%), reflecting the added cost of spike noise and real-time dynamics.

\subsection{Diminishing response updates}
Overall fit quality, as measured by RMSE, summarizes how closely a model's responses match human responses on average, but it can obscure whether a model reproduces specific dynamical signatures that characterize how people actually integrate evidence \cite{palminteri2017falsification, wilson2019ten}. For example, a model could achieve a low aggregate error while failing to capture qualitative features of the underlying process, such as perseveration \cite{katahira2018statistical}, confirmation bias \cite{palminteri2017confirmation, palminteri2022computational}, or sequential choice-history effects \cite{yu2008sequential, zhang2014sequential}. We therefore also examined behavioral signatures that reliably emerged in the human data but were not directly targeted by the RMSE fitting procedure, providing a stronger test of whether our model's mechanisms (and not just its output) capture the underlying cognitive processes.

We first investigated the changes that participants made in their responses from observation to observation, specifically whether the magnitude of response change varied as participants integrated more evidence within a trial. For each participant, we computed $\Delta R$, the absolute change between one response and the next, and averaged this value across all trials at each point in the sequence (see Materials and Methods); the population-level trajectory shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}A--C (gray) is the median of these trajectories across participants. $\Delta R$ reliably declined as participants accumulated more evidence within a trial: a per-participant Spearman rank correlation between $\Delta R$ and observation number was significantly negative ($p<0.05$) for 85\% of participants in Binary Integration, 59\% in Continuous Integration, and 68\% in Value Integration; Proportion Inference did not show this pattern (see Supplementary Text). Among models, DPE, SNN, and PR all reproduced this declining trend (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}A--C, colors). In contrast, LI's $\Delta R$ remained nearly constant across observations due to its fixed learning rate, while Mean always exhibited the same steep decline due to having no free parameters.

The observed decline in $\Delta R$ resembled the power-law form assumed by DPE. To quantify this pattern, we fit a power-law curve ($A n^{-\lambda}$) directly to each participant's mean $\Delta R$ as a function of observation number via nonlinear least squares, and tested whether the fitted discounting rate $\lambda$ was significantly different from zero (see Materials and Methods). The null hypothesis was rejected for 98\% of participants in Binary Integration, 77\% in Continuous Integration, and 72\% in Value Integration. The fitted discounting rate $\lambda$ varied substantially across individuals (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}D--F). What's more, this individual variation was reliable both within task and (to a lesser extent) across the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}, see Supplementary Text). Across all three tasks, most participants showed a recency bias ($\lambda<1$), consistent with prior reports in similar sequential-estimation paradigms \cite{yoo2025people, kalm2018visual, baddeley2022recency, feher2017exploration}, but a sizable minority showed either near-optimal integration or a slight primacy bias.

\subsection{Noisy internal representations}
We also examined variability in participants' responses when repeatedly presented with the same evidence sequence. Genuine trial-to-trial variability under exact repetition must reflect noise in mental representations, computations, or responses, but the cognitive and neural mechanism of this noise remains unclear. Prior work analyzed response variability in the Proportion Inference task and argued that only models that feature compounding state noise could explain human behavior \cite{prat2024imprecise}. We extended these methods to the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks by ensuring that the initial observations in each trial recurred multiple times across trials, then defined $\sigma$ as each participant's average response variability under exact sequence repetition (see Materials and Methods). $\sigma$ varied substantially across individuals in each task (Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}A--C), and this variation was highly reliable both within task and across the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks, indicating that some individuals consistently represent, update, and/or report their beliefs more precisely than others, regardless of the specific evidence being integrated (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}).

In addition to measuring $\sigma$ across all repeated sequences, we characterized how $\sigma$ grew within a repeated sequence (see Materials and Methods). Human $\sigma$ grew substantially larger as more of the repeated sequence was observed (1.3--1.9$\times$ from first to last observation, Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}D--F, gray). This compounding growth in response variability suggests that noise persists within an internal, evolving state representation, rather than arising anew (independently) each time a response is generated. We also analyzed the autocorrelation of participants' residual responses to a repeated sequence, as a function of lag (see Materials and Methods). Human residuals were strongly and positively correlated at short lags ($r$=0.40--0.62 at lag 1), decaying gradually as lag increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}G--I, gray), indicating that this state noise persists across trials but is gradually overwritten as additional, independent noise accumulates.

The models introduced so far produce a single, deterministic response to any given evidence sequence, and therefore cannot immediately account for the response variability just described. A standard solution is to introduce noise at the point of response generation, either by selecting among possible responses probabilistically \cite{sutton2018reinforcement} or by adding independent noise directly to a model's continuous-valued response \cite{nassar2010approximately, piray2021model}. We implemented the latter for each of our mathematical models (Mean, LI, PR, and DPE) by adding independent Gaussian noise to each model's predicted response and fitting each participant's noise magnitude (minimizing negative log-likelihood, Eq.~\ref{eq:nll}; see Materials and Methods). Out of all the models tested, only SNN reproduced both the growth in $\sigma$ ($2.0$--$3.2\times$) and the decaying positive autocorrelation ($r$=0.69--0.76 at lag 1); the four noise-augmented math models showed no such trend in either measure (growth $0.93$--$1.15\times$; autocorrelation $\approx0$; Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}D--I, colors). This dissociation confirms that these dynamic behavioral signatures reflect genuine state noise, which naturally arises from spiking activity in SNN's persistent internal representations, but is absent from response-noise-augmented models. These findings are consistent with a noisy-state-based model that explains response variability in the Proportion Inference task \cite{prat2024imprecise}, but SNN grounds this noise in biologically plausible mechanisms and generalizes it across diverse tasks.

\subsection{Neural network predictions}
To further link the above behavioral phenomenon to the underlying neural mechanisms, and to generate predictions that could validate or falsify our model, we next ran several simulated experiments using SNN in the Continuous Integration task. We began by simulating an outlier paradigm, in which SNN observed a run of three consistent observations followed by one surprising outlier (e.g. 49, 50, 51, 60). Following the outlier, the decoded prediction error (PE) rose sharply before decaying back toward baseline prior to the next observation (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}A). Both the peak PE response and its subsequent decay rate (the relative decline in PE by the end of the outlier's window, per second; see Materials and Methods) scaled with the network's synaptic learning rate $\alpha_0$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}B; peak: $r$=0.91; decay rate: $r$=0.95; both $p$<0.0001). Consequently, the peak PE response strongly predicted the rate at which the PE signal subsequently decayed (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}C; $r$=0.93, $p$<0.0001).

We then asked how the discounting rate $\lambda$ influences SNN's spiking activity and $\Delta R$. We simulated SNN across a range of $\lambda$ values and measured ``activity decay'' (the percent decline in error-selective spiking activity from the first to the last observation within a trial; see Materials and Methods; Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}D). Both this neural activity decay and the network's behavioral $\Delta R$ decay (the percent decline in $\Delta R$ from the first two to the last two observations within a trial) became steeper as $\lambda$ increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}E; activity decay: $r$=0.89; $\Delta R$ decay: $r$=0.92; both $p$<0.0001). A decline in neural activity therefore predicted a decline in response change as more evidence was integrated within a trial (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}F; $r$=0.86, $p$<0.0001).

Finally, we tested whether spike noise in SNN is a direct source of behavioral response variability $\sigma$. Qualitatively, SNN's value estimate tracked the ideal target more closely in larger networks than in smaller ones, both within and across trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}G). To quantify this relationship while controlling for value drift before the current observation, we simulated another outlier experiment and computed two noise measures: ``PE noise', a signal-to-noise ratio for the error signal (see Materials and Methods) and $\sigma$. We repeated this experiment for multiple network sizes and random sequences, and found that both PE noise and $\sigma$ declined as network size increased (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}H; PE noise: $r$=-0.82; $\sigma$: $r$=-0.86; both $p$<0.0001). PE noise thus directly predicted $\sigma$ across network sizes (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}I; $r$=0.82, $p$<0.0001), confirming that spiking noise in error-selective neurons is a direct source of behavioral response variability (see Supplementary Text for a second, decoder-free reliability metric that corroborates this relationship).

\subsection{Perturbation dissociates synaptic and recurrent memory}
Having established a neural mechanism that jointly accounts for behavioral and neural signatures of evidence integration, we asked whether these conclusions depend on the specific circuit implementation of DPE, or whether they generalize to a different neural substrate. Our SNN implementation of DPE maintains its running estimate $V_n$ through persistent recurrent activity in the \textit{value} population, consistent with one leading account of how the brain sustains information in working memory \cite{wang2002probabilistic, wang2008decision}. An influential alternative hypothesis, however, proposes that working memory can instead be maintained by synaptic plasticity, without requiring sustained spiking to hold information between observations \cite{mongillo2008synaptic, stokes2015activity}. These two candidate mechanisms can both plausibly implement DPE and are difficult to distinguish from behavior alone. We investigated which neural mechanism is more plausible using a synaptic variant of SNN. This network replaces \textit{value}'s persistent recurrent activity with a synaptic mechanism: a dopamine-like error-driven learning rule instead stores the running estimate $V_n$ in the weights of plastic synapses, updating it only in response to the prediction error signal (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}A). These plastic synapses connect a new \textit{etc} population (which fires at a constant, non-selective rate) to \textit{value}, driving it according to a learned transformation (see Materials and Methods).

The synaptic model also captured individual human behavior on the Continuous Integration task, but performed slightly worse than the recurrent model (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}B; Wilcoxon $p$<0.0001). To further dissociate the two neural mechanisms, we ran a simulated experiment with a causal perturbation, then compared each model's decoded value trajectory. We injected noise into the \textit{value} population during the inter-trial interval (ITI; see Materials and Methods), mimicking a distractor task or non-invasive brain stimulation delivered during a working-memory delay. Example single-trial dynamics show that both models' decoded estimates are disrupted while the perturbation is active, but only the recurrent model's disruption persists once the ITI ends (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}C). Across a range of perturbation strengths, the recurrent model's error (RMSE, relative to the running mean) and response variability ($\sigma$) both grew reliably with perturbation strength (RMSE: $r$=0.77; $\sigma$: $r$=0.58; both $p$<0.0001), while the synaptic model showed no such relationship (both $r\approx0$, $p$>0.9) (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}D), confirming that only the recurrent, persistent-activity-based mechanism is vulnerable to a transient disruption of ongoing neural activity.

\section{Discussion}

People constantly form and revise beliefs from streams of uncertain evidence: they estimate hidden quantities from repeated observations, judge how much to trust a source over repeated interactions, and track the feedback from repeated actions. Given the complexity and prevalence of evidence integration, it is not surprising that individuals solve this problem in markedly different ways. To understand the computational and neural basis of individual variability, we proposed the Discounted Prediction Error model (DPE) and tested it across four distinct evidence integration tasks, two from prior work and two developed for this study. DPE captured not only average human behavior but also the substantial individual differences in how people weight new evidence against prior belief. We further characterized this variability through two behavioral signatures of the integration process: the rate at which people discount older evidence ($\lambda$, which reveals whether people exhibit a primacy or recency bias) and the variability of people's responses ($\sigma$, which reveals how consistently people represent and update their beliefs). Both $\sigma$ and $\lambda$ varied substantially between individuals, indicating that people differ not only in the conclusions they reach but in their cognitive strategies and mental precision. Compared to other prediction-error-based integration models, we argued that DPE's relative simplicity allows for generalization across tasks and a tractable circuit-level implementation. We defended these claims by realizing DPE in a biologically plausible spiking neural network (SNN) and applying it across our diverse task suite. This SNN reproduced human behavior and individual differences, recreated the growth and persistence of $\sigma$ across repeated sequences, and yielded several concrete, falsifiable predictions linking neural activity to behavior. Together, these results suggest that a discounted error-driven mechanism, implemented in spiking neurons, offers a plausible and generative account of both the shared and idiosyncratic ways people integrate evidence over time.

\subsection{Individual differences in discounting and precision}
Studying evidence integration through overall fit quality alone risks conflating models that reach similar predictions through different underlying processes; examining secondary behavioral signatures that emerge from a model's update mechanism provides a complementary test of whether it captures the underlying cognitive process, not merely its output \cite{palminteri2017falsification, wilson2019ten}. Such signatures have already proven useful in evidence integration tasks similar to ours: Yoo et al. \cite{yoo2025people} showed that response updates decline in magnitude as more evidence accumulates within a trial, revealing whether an individual shows a primacy or recency bias; and Prat-Carrabin and Woodford \cite{prat2024imprecise} showed that responses to an exactly repeated sequence vary systematically across repetitions, arguing that this reflects an internal, compounding state representation rather than noise added only at the point of response. However, each signature has only been examined within a single task, leaving open whether it reflects a general property of integration or an artifact of that task's design. We addressed this directly by measuring both signatures -- the discounting rate $\lambda$ and the response variability $\sigma$ -- across four tasks that deliberately varied along several relevant dimensions, including input type, sequence length, and estimated quantity.

The decline in response change magnitude ($\Delta R$) and the compounding response variability under repetition both generalized to our two new tasks, replicating and extending Yoo et al.'s \cite{yoo2025people} and Prat-Carrabin and Woodford's \cite{prat2024imprecise} findings. Furthermore, both $\lambda$ and $\sigma$ reveal genuine, stable individual differences rather than overfitting to a single task or dataset: split-half reliability was high for both parameters (Supplementary Text), indicating that, within a given task, most participants have a consistent bias towards primacy or recency and a consistent level of response precision. This consistency also extended across tasks: within-subject, an individual's $\sigma$, and to a lesser extent their $\lambda$, in one task predicted their value of that same parameter in the other, suggesting that both reflect fundamental, individualized signatures of evidence integration in the brain.

\subsection{A generative, extensible mechanism for evidence integration}
Human evidence integration has been explained by models across a wide range of complexity \cite{gallistel2014perception, zhu2020bayesian, degroot1974reaching, parpart2018heuristics, raviv2012recent, usher2001time, glaze2015normative, piray2021model, zhang2014sequential, cain2012computational, gibson1997learning, waskom2018decision, kopec2024integrate, prat2024imprecise}. What makes a model a plausible candidate for the brain's actual mechanism, rather than a redescription of its output, is whether its weighting of past evidence could reasonably be computed by the brain in real time. Numerous factors contribute to a model's neural plausibility, including its memory requirements, the number and complexity of the operations it performs, and whether its biases emerge from its mechanisms or are embedded in them by design. We argue that DPE meets all these criteria, whereas competing models fail at least one.

DPE belongs to a family of models that compress the influence of all past observations into a single estimate that is carried forward in time and updated via a weighted prediction error (PE; Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}). These models differ primarily in how the weight on the newest observation, $\alpha(n)$, is computed. In the simplest case, $\alpha(n)$ is held constant, as in LeakyIntegrator and various RL algorithms such as TD(0) \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. DPE is more flexible: $\alpha(n) = \alpha_0/n^\lambda$ changes with every observation, but only as a deterministic function of the observation count $n$ and the individually-fixed discounting rate $\lambda$. Adaptive-learning-rate models, including Kalman-filter-style Bayesian updating, instead compute $\alpha(n)$ online as a function of observed surprise or an evolving volatility estimate \cite{behrens2007learning, nassar2010approximately, yu2008sequential}. In every member of this family, bias is a byproduct of an otherwise generic attempt to track an uncertain, dynamic, or imprecisely-remembered world, not a pattern the model was built in advance to reproduce. For instance, DPE recovers the Bayes-optimal running mean at $\lambda=1$ and departs from it smoothly as $\lambda$ discounts older evidence more or less steeply. This kind of imprecise, approximate discounting with more observations is a reasonable computational constraint for a biological system, consistent with other work showing that weighted PE models can recreate both Bayesian-like behavior and the primacy and recency biases \cite{godara2025apparent}. The $\alpha(n)$ framework also extends well beyond volatility tracking: the same weighted-PE structure has been adapted to capture effects such as confirmation bias, by adjusting $\alpha(n)$ asymmetrically for confirming versus disconfirming evidence \cite{palminteri2022computational, palminteri2017confirmation}.

Competing models fail neural plausibility standards in different ways. PR's weight for each past observation must be recomputed across the entire raw history whenever a new observation arrives, requiring an expanding memory of all previous observations alongside an expensive weighted summation -- the price of the modest fit advantage PR showed on Continuous Integration (Table~\ref{tab:effect_sizes}). Furthermore, its two free parameters are chosen specifically to let the fitted curve bend into whatever primacy or recency shape the data show; this mechanism serves as a flexible measurement of an already-observed bias, in the same spirit as other descriptive weighting methods that recover a temporal profile without proposing how it could be computed online \cite{anderson1965primacy, levi2018strategic, okazawa2018psychophysical}. Similarly, the Experience-Weighted Attraction (EWA) model \cite{camerer1999experience, ho2007selftuning} was developed for repeated strategic interaction, where a player faces other agents over a defined strategy space and can weigh the foregone payoff of unchosen strategies; it therefore requires maintaining a memory of each strategy's attraction and foregone payoff, as well as a parallel computation across alternatives to select a response. The same memory problem arises within a purely Bayesian approach: exact online inference about a changing environment requires maintaining a full posterior over every possible changepoint location \cite{adams2007bayesian}, an unbounded representation that is intractable for a biological system and is precisely why more tractable delta-rule approximations were developed \cite{wilson2013mixture}. It is telling that, to our knowledge, none of these models (PR, EWA, or exact Bayesian tracking) has actually been realized in a neural circuit. In contrast, DPE's running estimate requires neither unbounded memory nor involved calculations, and its biases emerge rather than being assumed. Being neurally implementable is therefore not a side benefit of DPE's simplicity: it is the central argument for treating it as a candidate mechanism rather than a redescription of behavior.

Our task suite specifically targets evidence integration in which the generative statistics remain constant within a trial: this important class of problems has numerous real-world analogues such as estimating a batch's defect rate from a sample of inspections, or gauging a restaurant's typical quality from a handful of reviews. In this setting, DPE closely matched human behavior across all four tasks studied here, and generally outperformed other baseline models, including the Bayes-optimal Mean and the baseline LeakyIntegrator. However, more complicated evidence integration tasks may require more sophisticated models. DPE is a deliberately-simple starting point, not a ceiling on what dynamic PE models can achieve. Critically, because the core neural network responsible for the PE update is agnostic about how $\alpha(n)$ is computed, additional neural populations could be added alongside the existing error circuitry, allowing the SNN to implement more sophisticated, data-driven modulation by $\alpha(n)$. This kind of compositional extension has already been demonstrated at scale in other NEF models, which combine many such populations to perform diverse, highly-complex cognitive functions \cite{choo2018spaun, dumont2023biologically}. Taken together, these features -- performance, scalability, heterogeneity, and extensibility -- make DPE a compelling account of the cognitive processes underlying evidence integration.

\subsection{Novelty and predictions of the neural network}
Implementing a cognitive model in a neural network offers several advantages beyond behavioral or computational-level analysis alone. It provides an existence proof that the computations required by a mathematical model can be carried out in a biologically plausible network, rather than remaining an abstract description, and grounds individual differences in concrete biological mechanisms. A neural implementation also generates falsifiable predictions about neural activity that purely mathematical models cannot, since the latter make no claims about how their computations are physically realized.

PE signals are a well-established neural motif, observed across dopaminergic reward learning \cite{glimcher2011understanding}, predictive coding in perceptual inference, and motor control, making this mechanism a strong candidate for evidence integration in the brain. Notably, PE signals also contribute to learning in value-neutral tasks like ours \cite{costa2025striatal}, and many studies have identified neural signatures of dynamic learning rates \cite{doll2011dopaminergic, schmidt2014mind, farashahi2017metaplasticity}, closely resembling the dynamic weighting DPE computes via $\alpha(n)$. Despite this, previous studies have rarely used neural network models to investigate dynamic PE-based evidence integration. Most existing models use Kalman-filter-style Bayesian updating \cite{millidge2021neural, adamiat2025spiking}, and have only been validated against synthetic tracking or regression tasks, never against real behavioral data. To our knowledge, our network is the first biologically-plausible, PE-based implementation that reproduces human behavior (especially individual differences) in evidence integration.

We leveraged our SNN to generate four testable predictions relating neural activities to observable behavioral measures, then explained how each prediction arises from a specific biological mechanism. Importantly, all of these measures are either directly observable (population spiking activity recorded with high-temporal-resolution methods, response change and variability, etc.) or recoverable using well-established decoding techniques from the neural literature (population-vector decoding \cite{georgopoulos1986neuronal, wu2006bayesian}). Note that all four effects emerge from our particular circuit implementation, and are not a direct mathematical consequence of the DPE equation itself, making them network-specific, falsifiable predictions.

First, following a surprising observation, the size of a population's transient PE response predicts how quickly that response subsequently decays back to baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}A--C). Because $\alpha(n)$ scales both the magnitude of the error response and the speed with which it drives PE updates, individuals with a stronger baseline learning rate ($\alpha_0$) will both react more strongly and adapt faster to unexpected observations (outliers). This can be tested by decoding spikes from error-sensitive neurons during the cue period and correlating the maximum PE response to the subsequent PE decay before the next cue.

Second, the firing-rate decline of error-selective neurons within a trial predicts the decay rate of response updates (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}D--F). Because $\alpha(n)$ scales the dynamic firing rate of error neurons that in turn discount value updates, individuals with higher discounting $\lambda$ will show progressively weaker error responses and smaller response updates as more evidence accumulates within a trial. This can be tested by measuring the activity of error-sensitive neurons after each observation and correlating it with the magnitude of response updates.

Third, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the decoded error signal predicts the resulting behavioral response variability $\sigma$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}G--I). The error population's representational capacity -- and thus how consistently it supports integration -- is shaped by biological factors such as the number of neurons recruited to represent the signal. This can be tested by measuring either spike-based or signal-based PE SNR (see Supplementary Text) and correlating it with $\sigma$ over repeated sequences.

Fourth, perturbing the network during the intertrial interval (ITI) increases subsequent response error and variability in a dose-dependent way for networks that rely on activity-dependent working memory, but not for networks that rely on synaptic (activity-silent) working memory (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}C--D). The recurrent circuit maintains the running estimate in ongoing spiking activity that a delay-period perturbation can corrupt, while the synaptic circuit stores it in synaptic weights a transient current cannot reach. This can be tested by applying a distractor task or non-invasive brain stimulation during the ITI and asking whether decoding accuracy recovers once the delay ends. More broadly, whether the brain relies on persistent, resource-intensive working memory or on more efficient, plasticity-based strategies is a question that extends well beyond evidence integration -- some have argued the brain shifts from the former to the latter as task demands increase \cite{collins2012much, westbrook2025striatal}. A similar synaptic-versus-working-memory comparison, in reward learning during foraging, reached a conclusion resembling ours: mechanisms relying on persistent activity were more sensitive to external perturbation than synaptic ones \cite{pereira2024brain}.

Four key features distinguish our SNN from the recurrent neural networks (RNN) that are typically fitted to human behavior: an anatomically interpretable architecture, biologically plausible single-neuron dynamics, a compositional structure that supports extension to new tasks, and inherent representational noise. Our network is built from distinct populations that each perform specific functional computations, such as calculating discounted PE, maintaining the running value estimate, or tracking elapsed observations. This structure makes our network's predictions directly comparable to recordings from functionally or anatomically distinct cortical areas. An RNN's undifferentiated hidden layer, by contrast, mixes these computations together with no natural mapping onto specific brain regions or cell types, and requires post-hoc decomposition to identify the active representations and computations.

Many RNN architectures commonly used to fit behavioral choice data, such as gated recurrent units \cite{jian2025discovering}, rely on individual ``neurons'' that perform several nonlinear gating operations at once, a level of computational complexity implausible for a single biological neuron. Our network instead uses simple leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, widely regarded as a reasonable compromise between biological realism and computational tractability \cite{eliasmith2013build, gerstner2014neuronal, dayan2005theoretical}.

The NEF's compositional approach to unified cognitive modelling can be used to build large-scale models spanning many distinct cognitive tasks from a shared set of reusable neural components \cite{choo2018spaun}. An RNN, by contrast, typically must be retrained from scratch for each new task, since nothing in its architecture encourages its representations to be generalizable across tasks.

Spiking noise in our network is an emergent, unavoidable consequence of representing a continuous value in a finite population of spiking neurons: population size, neural gain, and tuning-curve heterogeneity all combine to set its magnitude. Each of these parameters is a biologically meaningful, independently measurable quantity rather than a free parameter fit only to match behavior. An RNN's noise, if included at all, must instead be injected or fit as a free parameter with no such interpretation, making any match to human $\sigma$ dynamics an artifact of fitting rather than an emergent property of the network's representation. While RNNs have achieved excellent fits to behavioral data in their own right, these features make our SNN a fundamentally different kind of model -- one built to generate anatomically- and mechanistically-grounded neural predictions, rather than simply to maximize behavioral fit.

\subsection{Limitations}
Several open questions remain about the discounting and precision analyses above. Unlike the other three tasks, Prat-Carrabin and Woodford's original Proportion Inference task showed no reliable decline in $\Delta R$ (Supplementary Text). Neither repetition itself nor the brevity of the sequence, considered alone, explains this exception. One possibility is that this short, binary evidence stream does not engage the same gradual confidence-accumulation process that the other three, longer tasks recruit. Another possibility is that asking participants to infer the value of an unknown quantity (especially with fewer samples) invites a categorically different strategy than the continuous, incrementally-diminishing updates that our models predict. Relatedly, $\lambda$'s cross-task transfer was statistically modest: the discounting rate correlated only weakly between Binary and Continuous Integration. While we suspect that increasing the sample size (more participants, more trials, and/or more observations) would reveal individually-stable, task-independent discounting biases more robustly, we acknowledge that different tasks may inspire distinct discounting strategies.

Our task suite could be expanded in several ways. All four tasks feature generative statistics that remain constant within a trial, so we cannot address whether a more complex model would outperform DPE in tasks involving genuine environmental change (e.g., a dynamic shift in the mean). Relatedly, because DPE recovers Mean and LeakyIntegrator as special cases of its parameter space (Supplementary Text), its flexibility can make it difficult to conclusively determine whether DPE itself or one of these simpler nested models better describes human behavior. More broadly, all four tasks belong to a similar sequential-estimation paradigm, and the generalizability of DPE and its neural implementation beyond this kind of task remains to be tested.  Finally, none of the neural predictions above have yet been tested against real neural data: no spiking, imaging, or perturbation recordings exist for these tasks to confirm or falsify them, so each of our predictions remains prospective.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Cognitive tasks}

\subsubsection*{Proportion Inference task}
Participants ($n=21$) viewed a box containing red and green rings and, after each of 5 sequentially-revealed rings, moved a slider (0\% to 100\% red) to report their current estimate of the proportion of red rings in the box \cite{prat2024imprecise}. Each participant completed 200 such trials. Because each trial's binary sequence was drawn independently from only $2^5=32$ possible sequences, many trials recurred exactly for a given participant, allowing responses to the same preceding sequence to be compared across repeated presentations. Note that, unlike the other tasks, this task asked participants to estimate something about an unknown distribution (the proportion of balls in the box), rather than summarize the sequence up to the current observation.

\subsubsection*{Binary Integration task}
Participants ($n=46$) viewed a sequence of red and blue balls, presented one at a time, and moved a slider after each observation to report their running estimate of the proportion of blue balls observed so far. Each of 32 trials consisted of 15 observations drawn from an independently-sampled target proportion. As in the Proportion Inference task, a subset of trials coincidentally shared identical initial observations by chance, allowing for a repeated-sequence comparison up to the fifth observation.

\subsubsection*{Continuous Integration task}
Participants ($n=46$) viewed a sequence of numbers, presented one at a time and each drawn from a hidden normal distribution (mean ranging from 15 to 85, fixed standard deviation of 10), and moved a slider after each observation to report their running estimate of the sequence's average. Each of 32 trials consisted of 15 observations. To ensure the repetition of sequences required to calculate $\sigma$, the first 4 observations of every trial were deliberately drawn from a shared pool of 8 fixed sequences, each repeated across 4 trials, producing designed rather than coincidental repetition at the beginning of each sequence.

\subsubsection*{Value Integration task}
Participants ($n=38$) viewed pairs of snack images, presented sequentially on opposite sides of the screen, and moved a joystick-controlled slider to report their running estimate of the average value difference between the left and right snacks \cite{yoo2025people}. Snacks were rated for individual desirability by each participant beforehand. Each of 15 trials included 30 such image pairs; sequences were never repeated within or across participants.

\subsection{Participants}
Participants for the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks were recruited through Prolific, restricted to an approval rating of at least 90\%, more than 20 previously completed studies, and fluent English. 79 and 78 participants completed the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks, respectively (a small number of additional sessions were terminated early due to repeated response timeouts or failure to finish all trials). After applying the exclusion criterion described below, 46 participants who completed both tasks were retained. All participants provided informed consent electronically, reading a series of disclosures and checking a box affirming they had read and agreed to participate before beginning, under a protocol approved by the Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects at Dartmouth College. Participants were compensated \$5 for completing each task, plus up to an additional \$5 based on task performance, with a median completion time of approximately 41 minutes per task, corresponding to an effective hourly wage of approximately \$7--15 depending on performance.

We excluded participants whose responses did not reliably depend on observations prior to the most recent one within a trial (``non-integrators''), since such responses provide no sign of evidence integration, regardless of the integration strategy used or the accuracy of their final responses. This single test catches participants who exhibit a variety of disengaged response patterns (e.g., reporting whatever observation they just saw), without penalizing participants who integrate evidence inaccurately or with unusual weighting. For each participant and task, we regressed each response on the current observation's value and the expanding mean of all strictly prior observations within that trial, fit per trial and aggregated across trials. We constructed a 95\% confidence interval on the prior-observations coefficient via a trial-level cluster bootstrap (resampling trials with replacement, 20,000 resamples), and retained a participant only if this interval excluded zero. Participants with fewer than 8 trials, or fewer than 10 total observations, in a task were excluded outright as too little data to test reliably. This criterion was applied per task, but a participant excluded in either task was excluded from both, so the same 46 participants are analyzed in both Binary and Continuous Integration.

\subsection{Computational models}
We compared DPE (Eqs.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}--\ref{eq:dpe_alpha}) against three baseline models.

\subsubsection*{Mean}
Mean computes the unweighted running average of all observations so far:
\begin{equation}
    V_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n I_i
\end{equation}
Mean has no free parameters, and its output is implicitly bounded to the valid response range $[-1,1]$ by construction.

\subsubsection*{LeakyIntegrator (LI)}
LI applies the same error-driven update as DPE, but with a constant learning rate $\alpha_{LI}$ rather than one that decays with $n$:
\begin{equation}
    V_n = V_{n-1} + \alpha_{LI}\left(I_n - V_{n-1}\right)
\end{equation}
LI's single free parameter is $\gamma \in [0,1]$, with $\alpha_{LI} = 1-\gamma$; LI's update was clipped to $[-1,1]$ after each observation. LI is thus equivalent to DPE with $\lambda=0$ and $\alpha_0 = 1-\gamma$: a constant, non-decaying learning rate.

\subsubsection*{PrimacyRecency (PR)}
PR computes a weighted average of all observations using a temporal weighting function \cite{pooley2011understanding, galdo2022quest}:
\begin{equation}
    V_n = \frac{\sum_{o=1}^n w(o,n)\, I_o}{\sum_{o=1}^n w(o,n)}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    w(o,n) = \left[1 - \left(1-\varepsilon_p^{o}\right)\left(1-\varepsilon_r^{\,n-o+1}\right)\right](1-\eta) + \eta
\end{equation}
PR's two free parameters, $\varepsilon_p, \varepsilon_r \in [0,1]$, control primacy and recency weighting respectively; $\eta=0.01$ is fixed \cite{yoo2025people}. PR's output is implicitly bounded to $[-1,1]$ by construction.

\subsubsection*{Proportion Inference response transform}
On the Proportion Inference task, every model's raw estimate $V_n$ was additionally rescaled before comparison to human responses:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:laplace}
    V_n \to V_n \cdot \frac{n}{n+2}
\end{equation}
a Laplace-smoothing correction equivalent to the optimal Bayesian estimate of a probability under a uniform prior (adding one pseudo-observation of each outcome before dividing), reflecting this task's distinct estimand (see Cognitive Tasks).

\subsubsection*{Response noise variants}
To investigate each math model's ability to reproduce trial-to-trial response variability, and to validate model rankings with an independent fit metric, we defined a noise-augmented variant of each of the four models above (Mean, LI, PR, and DPE): independent Gaussian noise, $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{resp}^2)$, was added to that model's deterministic response and clipped to $[-1,1]$, with $\sigma_{resp}$ fit as an additional free parameter per participant (see Parameter fitting).

\subsection{Neural network model}

\subsubsection*{Neural Engineering Framework}
In the Neural Engineering Framework (NEF), spiking activity within a population of neurons represents cognitive states such as value estimates, prediction errors, or weights. These states are defined as vectors that change with the network's spiking activity, and we can decode these vectors from the underlying spiking activity, or encode new vectors into spiking activity by driving neurons with an external current. Together, encoding and decoding define the concept of representation in an NEF network, and the framework provides equations that quantify how these processes occur biologically. A key aspect of representation in the NEF is population coding: each neuron in a population is sensitive to the represented state to a different degree, specified by that neuron's random tuning curve, so only by decoding from many neurons together can a reliable estimate of the represented quantity be recovered.

Functionally, the NEF can implement any linear or non-linear dynamical system in a network of spiking neurons, effectively compiling a mathematical description of a cognitive computation (here, Eqs.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}--\ref{eq:dpe_alpha}) into a network that implements it. To transform represented states into new states, the NEF specifies synaptic connection weights between (or within) populations. For example, \textit{error} receives one connection carrying the current observation $I_n$ and another carrying \textit{value}'s estimate with a negative transform, so that \textit{error} comes to represent their difference, $I_n - V_{n-1}$. Its connection to \textit{value}, in turn, is specified with a nonlinear function that computes the product of \textit{error}'s two represented dimensions, $\alpha(n) \times (I_n-V_{n-1})$.

Critically, although the NEF provides tools for representing and transforming cognitive states, it does not dictate a unique network architecture: many configurations of neural populations and synaptic connections can implement the same high-level computation. Specifying a network architecture therefore amounts to a hypothesis about which brain areas represent which cognitive states, and how they are connected to perform the required transformations. This is exactly the hypothesis under comparison between the recurrent and synaptic implementations described below. For a more thorough introduction to the NEF, and examples of the range of cognitive and neural models built using it, see \cite{eliasmith2013build, choo2018spaun, dumont2023biologically, duggins2022constructing, duggins2024spiking, dumont2022}.

\subsubsection*{Recurrent implementation}
We implemented DPE (Eqs.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}--\ref{eq:dpe_alpha}) as a spiking neural network (SNN) using the NEF. The network comprises populations of spiking leaky-integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons connected via current-based synapses, two mechanisms widely regarded as a compromise between biological realism and computational efficiency \cite{eliasmith2013build, duggins2022constructing, gerstner2014neuronal, dayan2005theoretical}. In its default (recurrent) configuration, the network comprises three main populations (Fig.~\ref{fig:models_overview}B): \textit{error}, \textit{value}, and a counting network, \textit{count} (described below). \textit{Error} is two-dimensional, representing the discounting weight $\alpha(n)$ (received from \textit{count}) and the raw prediction error $I_n - V_{n-1}$ (received from the current observation and feedback from \textit{value}). A single connection from \textit{error} to \textit{value} computes the product of these two represented dimensions, $\alpha(n)(I_n-V_{n-1})$ (the weighted prediction error of Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}), and adds it into \textit{value}'s input. \textit{Value} is one-dimensional and represents the running estimate $V_n$. In this recurrent implementation, \textit{value} maintains $V_n$ via a recurrent connection (synaptic time constant $\tau_{fb}=0.2$s), so that $V_n$ persists as ongoing recurrent activity between observations.

Each observation was presented as an input pulse for $t_{obs}=1.5$s, followed by an inter-trial interval (ITI) of $t_{iti}=0.5$s. During the ITI, the input was silenced, and \textit{error} was inhibited in one of two ways to prevent \textit{value} from converging toward a nonsensical $I_n=0$. By default, \textit{error}'s neurons were inhibited directly, fully blocking its activity until the next observation. For the perturbation experiment (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}), we instead inhibited an intermediate \textit{gate} population projecting \textit{value}'s feedback to \textit{error}, allowing \textit{error} to continue driving learning throughout the ITI. We verified that the choice of inhibition mechanism made little difference to the network's overall dynamics. Model responses were decoded from \textit{value}'s spiking activity 0.5s into each observation period. By default, \textit{error} and \textit{value} each contain 500 neurons and \textit{count} contains 2500 neurons.

\subsubsection*{Synaptic implementation}
An alternative implementation replaces \textit{value}'s persistent recurrent activity with a synaptic mechanism (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}A). Rather than a recurrent connection, \textit{value} instead receives input from a new population, \textit{etc}, which fires at a constant, non-selective rate; its activity carries no information about $I_n$ or $V_n$. As in the recurrent implementation, \textit{error}'s access to \textit{value}'s feedback was silenced during the ITI (see above). \textit{Etc} comprised 500 neurons, the same as \textit{error} and \textit{value}. The connection from \textit{etc} to \textit{value} is plastic, initialized to zero and updated online via the Prescribed Error Sensitivity (PES) rule \cite{macneil2011fine}, a biologically motivated, spike-based error-driven learning rule capable of learning weights that compute a wide range of functions \cite{voelker2015solution}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pes}
    \Delta\omega_{ij} = \kappa\, a_i\, (\epsilon_j \cdot E_n)
\end{equation}
where $\omega_{ij}$ is the weight between presynaptic neuron $i$ (in \textit{etc}) and postsynaptic neuron $j$ (in \textit{value}), $\kappa=4\times10^{-4}$ is a fixed learning rate, $a_i$ is the activity of presynaptic neuron $i$, $\epsilon_j$ is the encoder for postsynaptic neuron $j$, and $E_n$ is the same weighted prediction error $\alpha(n)(I_n-V_{n-1})$ decoded from \textit{error}. Because \textit{etc}'s spiking pattern carries no information, the entire estimate $V_n$ is encoded in the synaptic weights $\omega_{ij}$ themselves, rather than in any ongoing spiking activity: an ``activity-silent'' storage mechanism \cite{mongillo2008synaptic, stokes2015activity}.

\subsubsection*{Counting network}
Both the recurrent and synaptic implementations rely on the same counting network, \textit{count}, to track the observation index $n$ and decode the discounting weight $\alpha(n)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_alpha}) that modulates \textit{error}. To detect the onset of each new observation, \textit{count} performs approximate differentiation on the raw input stream: an \textit{onset detector} population receives the input through two parallel connections, one with a fast synaptic time constant ($\tau_{fast}=0.01$s) and a positive sign, and one with a slow time constant ($\tau_{slow}=0.2$s) and a negative sign. Because \textit{onset detector}'s neurons are tuned to fire only for positive input, the population emits a brief pulse only on a rising edge of the input, i.e., when a new observation begins, not during the sustained presentation of an observation or during the ITI. These pulses drive a \textit{memory} population, which integrates them over time via a recurrent connection (synapse $\tau_{fb}=0.2$s), accumulating a running count of the observations presented so far. Two linear readouts decode the observation count $n$ and the discounting weight $\alpha(n)=\alpha_0/n^\lambda$ directly from that activity; the latter is what feeds into \textit{error}, as described above. Because $\alpha(n)$ is computed from \textit{memory}'s imprecise, noisy spiking activity rather than imposed externally, the resulting discounting schedule, and the behavioral and neural signatures that follow from it, emerges directly from the network's biophysics. By default, \textit{onset detector} contains 500 neurons and \textit{memory} contains 2000 neurons. Note that we rescale \textit{memory}'s representational radius to the maximum observation count for a given task to ensure accurate counting.

\subsection{Parameter fitting}
For each participant and task, we fit each model's free parameters using the tree-structured Parzen estimator sampler implemented in Optuna \cite{optuna_2019}. The loss was the root-mean-squared error (RMSE) between each model's prediction and the participant's response. To compute it, each participant's trials were randomly partitioned into $k=5$ disjoint folds, RMSE was computed separately within each fold, and these five values were averaged, producing an in-sample loss metric. We did not attempt genuine nested cross-validation due to the small number of trials per participant: a single held-out fold would be too small to reliably estimate RMSE.

To validate model rankings with an independent fit metric, we additionally fit each model's noise-augmented variant via negative log-likelihood (NLL). Each variant's deterministic response was computed once per Optuna trial, then an ensemble of 100 simulated responses was generated by independently redrawing that variant's Gaussian noise term each time. The loss was the Gaussian negative log-likelihood of the participant's observed response under the resulting per-observation ensemble mean and standard deviation (floored at $10^{-3}$ to keep the loss finite):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nll}
\text{NLL} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \left[\log\hat\sigma_t + \frac{(y_t - \hat\mu_t)^2}{2\hat\sigma_t^2}\right]
\end{equation}
where $y_t$ is the participant's response at observation $t$, and $\hat\mu_t$, $\hat\sigma_t$ are the ensemble mean and (floored) standard deviation at that same observation. As with RMSE, this loss was averaged across the same 5 folds.

Pairwise comparisons of RMSE distributions across models used two complementary approaches. First, for each task, we compared DPE's per-participant RMSE against each baseline model's RMSE using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (two-sided, $\alpha=0.05$; Fig.~\ref{fig:model_performance}). Second, for each task, we counted the number of participants for whom each model achieved the single lowest RMSE, restricted to participants with a valid fit for every model being compared (Table~\ref{tab:best_fit}).

\subsection{Discounting rate ($\lambda$)}
For each participant, we computed response change ($\Delta R$), the absolute difference between consecutive responses within a trial, and averaged this value across all trials at each observation, yielding one mean $\Delta R$ trajectory per participant per task (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}A--C). Proportion Inference was excluded from this and all following analyses in this section, since its $\Delta R$ trajectory does not decline systematically (see Supplementary Text). Participants with fewer than 3 defined points in their trajectory were excluded from all analyses below.

To test whether $\Delta R$ reliably declined within a trial, we computed the Spearman rank correlation between observation number $n$ and each participant's $\Delta R$ trajectory; a decline was flagged when this correlation was negative with $p<0.05$. To quantify the shape of this decline, we fit a power law, $A n^{-\lambda}$, to each participant's trajectory via bounded nonlinear least squares (initial guess $A=0.1$, $\lambda=0.5$; bounds $A,\lambda \in [0,2]$). To test whether the resulting discounting rate $\lambda$ was significantly different from zero, we used the standard error of $\lambda$ from the fit's covariance matrix in a two-sided $t$-test, with degrees of freedom equal to the number of points in that participant's trajectory minus 2. Participants whose fit landed within $10^{-3}$ of a parameter bound were excluded from this test, since the covariance estimate is unreliable at a constrained optimum.

We assessed $\lambda$'s reliability in two ways. Within task, we split each participant's trials into odd and even subsets, fit $\lambda$ separately within each half, and computed the Pearson correlation between the two resulting $\lambda$ estimates across participants. Across task, we compared each participant's $\lambda$ fit on the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks directly (Pearson correlation).

\subsection{Response variability ($\sigma$)}
To measure response variability under exact repetition, we first identified groups of trials, within a participant and task, that shared an identical sequence of initial observations (a ``repeated sequence''). For each repeated sequence, and separately at each shared observation within that sequence, we computed the standard deviation of the participant's responses across trials. Averaging this standard deviation across every observation and every repeated sequence yielded one $\sigma$ value per participant per task (Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}A--C). We only analyzed sequences that repeated at least 3 times, and only measured up to a maximum of 5 observations within a sequence.

To ensure repeated sequences for computing $\sigma$, the Continuous Integration task drew each trial's first 4 observations from one of 8 fixed prefixes, evenly centered across the task's mean range and each repeated across 4 of the 32 trials. Each trial was additionally assigned one of 32 target means, also evenly spread across the same range; trials' prefixes and target means were paired using the Hungarian algorithm, globally minimizing the total mismatch between a prefix's mean and its assigned target across all 32 trials jointly. Given a trial's prefix and target mean, the remaining 11 observations were drawn so that the full 15-observation sequence's sample mean landed on the target mean in expectation, with an analytic correction to the suffix's variance so the pooled sequence's expected standard deviation matched the task's fixed value despite the prefix being an independent block. If the achieved standard deviation fell outside tolerance, the suffix was redrawn (up to 100 attempts), and if that still failed for every repeat of a given prefix, the prefix itself was regenerated from scratch.

To measure how $\sigma$ grows within a repeated sequence (Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}D--F), we computed the same standard deviation as above, but separately at each observation rather than averaged across observations, and then averaged across participants at each observation. Each resulting trajectory was normalized by its value at its first available observation, so that every trajectory starts at 1 and growth is expressed relative to each source's baseline.

To measure the autocorrelation of residual response variability (Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}G--I), we first computed each response's residual: its deviation from that participant's mean response to the same sequence at the same observation. For each participant and lag $k$, we paired every residual at observation $t$ with the residual at observation $t+k$ within the same trial (pooling across all valid $t$ and across repeated sequences), and computed their Pearson correlation. We then averaged these per-participant correlations across participants at each lag.

We assessed $\sigma$'s reliability using the same two approaches as $\lambda$: within task, by splitting each participant's trials into interleaved odd and even subsets and recomputing $\sigma$ separately within each half (Pearson correlation between the two); and across task, by comparing each participant's $\sigma$ on the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks directly (Pearson correlation).

\subsection{Simulated neural network experiments}
Beyond fitting SNN to human data, we used it to generate additional, novel predictions linking its spiking dynamics to behavior. The four experiments in this section simulate SNN with artificial parameter combinations and synthetic input sequences, rather than any participant's fitted parameters or observed sequence, letting us systematically probe how each architectural parameter ($\alpha_0$, $\lambda$, network size) and implementation (recurrent vs.\ synaptic) shapes the network's behavioral and neural signatures, independent of any single participant's idiosyncratic fit. All experiments used the Continuous Integration task unless otherwise noted.

To test how the network's synaptic learning rate $\alpha_0$ shapes its transient response to a surprising observation (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}A--C), we simulated SNN's response to three roughly consistent observations (a cluster center $\pm$1 unit) followed by one outlier observation (the center $\pm$10 units). We swept $\alpha_0$ from 0.2 to 1.0 in steps of 0.1, holding $\lambda=0.7$ and network size fixed, and repeated each $\alpha_0$ value across 5 cluster centers (20, 35, 50, 65, and 80) and both outlier directions, averaging over 20 random seeds per combination. For each cell, we computed the peak PE response as the decoded $|PE|$ at a fixed benchmark time (the median peak latency across the full grid, rather than each cell's local peak), and its decay rate as the relative decrease from that peak to the end of the outlier's window, divided by the elapsed time.

To test how the discounting rate $\lambda$ jointly shapes error-selective spiking activity and behavioral response-change decay (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}D--F), we swept $\lambda$, drawing one value uniformly from $[0.1, 1.0]$ for each of 200 independent synthetic sessions (each a full 32-trial sequence, matching a real participant's session), holding $\alpha_0=0.7$ and network size fixed. For each session, we computed ``activity decay'' as the percent decrease in weight-tuned error-population activity (read out 0.5s into each observation) from the first to the last observation, and ``$\Delta R$ decay'' as the percent decrease in mean $\Delta R$ from the first two to the last two observations, both pooled across that session's 32 trials.

To test whether spiking noise from a finite network size impacts response variability, we first illustrate this qualitatively using one fixed example sequence at each network size (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}G). We then quantify it using the same trial design as the outlier experiment above (5 cluster centers, both outlier directions, 10 random seeds per combination), swept across 5 network sizes ($n_{neurons}=$ 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, applied to \textit{error}, \textit{value}, and \textit{onset detector}; \textit{memory} always using 4$\times$ more neurons), holding $\alpha_0=\lambda=0.7$ fixed (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}H--I). For each cell, we computed ``PE noise'' as the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean, as a percentage) of the decoded $|PE|$ trace within a fixed 200ms window centered on response time (0.4--0.6s into the outlier's presentation), and $\sigma$ as the standard deviation, across seeds, of the decoded response to that same outlier observation. A fully decoder-free complement to PE noise -- the split-half reliability of \textit{error}'s raw spike counts in the same response window -- is also computed from this same simulated experiment (see Supplementary Text).

To causally dissociate the recurrent and synaptic mechanisms' reliance on ongoing neural activity (Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}C--D), we simulated both SNN implementations on all 32 trials of each of the same 200 sessions used above. We injected a band-limited white-noise current (0--30Hz, RMS scaled by a strength parameter) into every neuron of the \textit{value} population during the ITI; \textit{error}, \textit{count}, and background populations were left unperturbed. We swept perturbation strength from 0 to 0.5 in steps of 0.1, holding $\alpha_0=\lambda=0.7$ fixed for both implementations. For each trial, we extracted the network's decoded response following the shared prefix's final observation and computed RMSE against that prefix's mean; $\sigma$ was computed as usual (the standard deviation of responses across repeated trials), but restricted to this same response and averaged across each session's 8 unique sequences. Both statistics were then averaged across sessions (mean $\pm$ SEM) separately at each perturbation strength.

\subsection*{Supplementary Text}

\subsubsection*{Relationship between Discounted Prediction Error, baseline models, and primacy/recency}

In the main text, we showed that humans reliably exhibit diminishing absolute response change, well characterized by a power-law decay curve, $\Delta R = A n^{-\lambda}$, with $\lambda$ varying substantially between individuals (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}D--F), which is consistent with our formulation of the Discounted Prediction Error model (DPE). Here, we show formally that setting the discounting rate $\lambda$ in DPE recreates two of the three baseline models introduced in Materials and Methods as special cases, then show formally how other values of $\lambda$ correspond to primacy and recency biases. For reference, DPE's update rule and learning-rate schedule (Eqs.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}--\ref{eq:dpe_alpha} in the main text) are:
\begin{equation*}
    V_n = V_{n-1} + \alpha(n) \bigl(I_n - V_{n-1}\bigr)
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    \alpha(n) = \frac{\alpha_0}{n^\lambda}
\end{equation*}

Setting $\lambda=0$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_alpha} gives $\alpha(n) = \alpha_0/n^0 = \alpha_0$ for every $n$, a constant learning rate that exactly reduces to the LeakyIntegrator (with $\alpha_{LI}=\alpha_0$). Similarly, setting $\alpha_0=1$ and $\lambda=1$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_alpha} gives $\alpha(n)=1/n$. Substituting directly into Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_update}:
\begin{equation}
V_n = V_{n-1} + \frac1n(I_n - V_{n-1}) = \frac{(n-1)V_{n-1} + I_n}{n}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the standard recursive form of the running mean: the new estimate weights the previous estimate by the $n-1$ observations it already summarizes, and the newest observation by 1. Unrolling this recursion (below) confirms every observation receives equal weight $1/n$, recovering Mean's running average, $V_n = \tfrac1n\sum_{i=1}^n I_i$, exactly.

More generally, setting $\lambda>1$ or $\lambda<1$ produces primacy or recency effects. To see this, unroll Eq.~\ref{eq:dpe_update} one step to expose the relative weight DPE assigns to the two most recent observations, $I_n$ and $I_{n-1}$:
\begin{equation}
V_n = \alpha(n)\,I_n + \bigl(1-\alpha(n)\bigr)\alpha(n-1)\,I_{n-1} + \bigl(1-\alpha(n)\bigr)\bigl(1-\alpha(n-1)\bigr)\,V_{n-2}
\end{equation}
so the ratio of $I_{n-1}$'s weight to $I_n$'s weight is
\begin{equation}
\rho(\lambda) = \frac{(1-\alpha(n))\,\alpha(n-1)}{\alpha(n)} = \left(1-\frac{\alpha_0}{n^\lambda}\right)\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\right)^{\lambda}
\end{equation}
A relative weight of $\rho(\lambda)>1$ means the earlier observation $I_{n-1}$ is weighted more than $I_n$ (primacy); $\rho(\lambda)<1$ means $I_n$ is weighted more (recency). To confirm $\rho$ depends monotonically on $\lambda$, differentiate its logarithm:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\log \rho(\lambda) = \frac{\alpha_0\,n^{-\lambda}\ln n}{1-\alpha_0\,n^{-\lambda}} + \ln\!\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\right)
\end{equation}
Both terms are strictly positive for any $n\geq2$ and $\alpha_0\in(0,1]$ (the first because $\alpha_0 n^{-\lambda}\in(0,1)$ and $\ln n>0$; the second because $n/(n-1)>1$), so $\rho(\lambda)$ is strictly increasing in $\lambda$. In other words, increasing the discounting rate always shifts weight toward earlier observations, decreasing it always shifts weight toward the current one, regardless of $\alpha_0$. The exact crossover, where $\rho(\lambda)=1$, satisfies $\alpha_0 = n^\lambda - (n-1)^\lambda$; at $\alpha_0=1$ this holds at $\lambda=1$ for every $n$, matching the special case above.

\subsubsection*{Model comparison via negative log-likelihood}
As an independent validation of the RMSE-based model comparison reported in the main text (Fig.~\ref{fig:model_performance}; Table~\ref{tab:best_fit}), we refit Mean, LeakyIntegrator (LI), PrimacyRecency (PR), and DPE using their noise-augmented variants (see Materials and Methods) and compared them via negative log-likelihood (NLL, Eq.~\ref{eq:nll}) instead of RMSE, across all four tasks (Fig.~\ref{fig:model_performance_nll}; paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test, two-sided).

The resulting ranking matches the RMSE-based comparison exactly. DPE had significantly lower NLL than all three baseline models on both Proportion Inference and Value Integration (all $p<0.0001$). On Binary and Continuous Integration, DPE again significantly outperformed Mean and LI (all $p<0.001$), but PR matched it on Binary Integration ($p=0.96$) and significantly outperformed it on Continuous Integration ($p<0.001$) -- the same pattern of wins, ties, and losses reported for RMSE. This consistency across two independent fit metrics, one measuring average prediction accuracy and the other the likelihood of the full response distribution, indicates that DPE's advantage over the baseline models does not depend on the particular loss function used to fit it.

\subsubsection*{Within- and across-task consistency of $\lambda$ and $\sigma$}
The fitted discounting rates $\lambda$ shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}D--F varied widely across participants and tasks. This variation was reliable for most participants within a given task: split-half (odd/even trial) correlations of fitted $\lambda$ were $r=0.78$ (Binary Integration), $r=0.77$ (Continuous Integration), and $r=0.92$ (Value Integration), all $p<10^{-9}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}A--B). Similarly, the response variability $\sigma$ shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}A--C varied widely across participants and tasks, but $\sigma$ was also reliable for most participants within a given task: split-half correlations of fitted $\sigma$ were $r=0.95$ (Proportion Inference), $r=0.62$ (Binary Integration), and $r=0.91$ (Continuous Integration), all $p<10^{-5}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}D--E). This provides initial support for the conclusion that $\lambda$ and $\sigma$ both measure stable individual traits related to evidence integration, rather than noise in a single session's fitting procedure.

One major advantage of developing the Binary and Continuous Integration tasks was their shared cross-task design: the same participants completed both tasks, which shared most features (instructions, tutorial, user interface, timing, response format). This allowed us to compare individual differences across tasks using a within-subjects design. We found that $\lambda$ was weakly but significantly correlated across tasks ($r=0.31$, $p=0.037$; Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}C), while $\sigma$'s cross-task correlation was considerably stronger ($r=0.70$, $p<10^{-7}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}F). This asymmetry suggests that response variability reflects a more stable, domain-general trait, whereas discounting rate reflects a mix of a stable trait and genuine task-specific integration strategy.

\subsubsection*{Response-change decay: the Proportion Inference exception}
Unlike the other three tasks, human $\Delta R$ in the Proportion Inference task does not reliably decline across the trial's 5 observations (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_balls}); only 9 of 21 participants show a negative slope across observations, consistent with a genuinely flat population trajectory rather than individual declines cancelling out in the aggregate. For this reason, we did not fit or analyze $\lambda$ in this task (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}D--F has no corresponding panel). Note that this flatness is not simply an artifact of the task's short trials: within 5 observations, Mean, PR, DPE, and SNN all predict a clear, substantial decline when fit to human data in this task, confirming that the task's generative statistics support diminishing updates over this observation window. The actual behavioral mechanism responsible for the human exception in this task remains unclear. It is plausible that this task's  short, binary evidence stream may not engage the same gradual confidence-accumulation process recruited by the other tasks' longer, more numerous observation sequences. This may be especially true when the stated objective is to infer an unknown rather than track the evidence seen thus far.

\subsubsection*{A decoder-free corroboration of the neurons-vs-SNR result}
The analysis of the relationship between neural and behavioral noise in the main text (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}H--I) depends on decoding a continuous PE signal from spiking activity, raising the question of whether its reliability genuinely reflects network size, or partly reflects some property of the decoders themselves. To test this, and to offer a neural noise metric that could be measured directly from spike recordings without requiring a trained decoder, we computed a fully decoder-free measure of reliability using the same simulated outlier experiment described in Materials and Methods: the split-half correlation of the \textit{error} population's raw spike counts. For each seed, we randomly split error-sensitive neurons into two halves 50 times, summed each half's spike counts into one time series, and correlated the two halves' time series (mean across seeds and splits). This split-half reliability increased monotonically with network size ($r=0.93$, $p<10^{-21}$), mirroring $\sigma$'s decline ($r=-0.86$, $p<10^{-15}$), and directly predicted $\sigma$ across network sizes ($r=-0.94$, $p<10^{-24}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:decoder_free}) -- an even tighter relationship than PE noise's covariance with $\sigma$ in the main text (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}I; $r=0.82$). Because this measure depends only on raw spiking activity, it corroborates the main text's PE-noise-vs-$\sigma$ relationship from an independent, strictly neural angle.
