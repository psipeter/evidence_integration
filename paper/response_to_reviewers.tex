We thank both reviewers for their careful and constructive evaluations of our
original submission. Their comments identified three gaps in that manuscript:
the case for our neural network as an advance over existing mathematical and
recurrent neural network models was underdeveloped; our reporting of model fit quality was neither
sufficiently transparent nor sufficiently central; and our neural analyses
stopped short of the quantitative, testable claims a network model should
support. The editor indicated that a revised manuscript should include
``additional experiments'' and address the reviewers' concerns. In response, we
designed and collected two new behavioral experiments, added four simulated
neural experiments that generate falsifiable predictions, and substantially
rewrote the manuscript.

Because several comments from the two reviewers concern the same material, we
first summarize the new results and arguments in a General response, then
address each comment individually, referring back to the relevant part. All
section, figure, equation, and table references refer to the revised
manuscript.

\section*{General response}

\subsection*{G1. Two new behavioral experiments}

We designed and collected two new tasks, Binary Integration and Continuous
Integration, completed by the same 46 participants. Each supplies something the
existing tasks did not: a within-subject design that tests whether individual
differences are stable traits rather than task-specific artifacts; trials long
enough (15 observations) to estimate a discounting rate reliably; and
deliberately repeated sequences, which make response variability measurable. The
manuscript now spans four tasks (Fig.~1), which vary in input type, sequence
length, repetition structure, and estimated quantity while sharing one response
format: a continuous, real-valued estimate reported after every observation.

\subsection*{G2. Three new behavioral findings}

\textbf{Response change declines within a trial.} The magnitude of response
change ($\Delta R$) declines within a trial, and does so reliably at the level of individual participants rather than only in
the aggregate: a per-participant rank correlation between $\Delta R$ and
observation number is significantly negative for 85\%, 59\%, and 68\% of
participants in Binary, Continuous, and Value Integration, and a fitted
power-law discounting rate $\lambda$ differs significantly from zero for 98\%,
77\%, and 72\% (Fig.~4). This decline is the behavioral signature of a learning
rate that decays as evidence accumulates; only models with the associated
mechanisms --- Discounted Prediction Error (DPE), PrimacyRecency (PR), and our
spiking neural network (SNN) ---
can reproduce individual variability in this signature.

\textbf{Response variability grows and persists under repetition.} Human
responses to an exactly repeated sequence grow more variable as the sequence
progresses (1.3--1.9$\times$ from first to last observation), and
response residuals are positively autocorrelated across consecutive responses
($r$~=~0.40--0.62 at lag~1; Fig.~5D--I). Only the SNN
reproduces these signatures (2.0--3.2$\times$; $r$~=~0.69--0.76), and it does so
without any fitted noise parameter; every alternative model we tested captures
neither (0.93--1.15$\times$; autocorrelation $\approx$~0) despite each being
given one fitted response noise parameter. This indicates that noise must reside
in a persistent, evolving state representation rather than arising during the response period.

\textbf{Discounting rate and response precision are stable individual traits.}
An individual's discounting rate ($\lambda$) and response precision ($\sigma$)
are both reliable within task (split-half $r$~=~0.77--0.92 and
$r$~=~0.62--0.95 respectively) and transfer across tasks ($\sigma$: $r$~=~0.70,
$p<10^{-7}$; $\lambda$: $r$~=~0.31, $p$~=~0.037; Fig.~S2), indicating that they
reflect stable individual traits. The weaker cross-task correlation of $\lambda$
suggests that this property may also reflect task-specific strategy.

\subsection*{G3. Model fits expanded and reported in full}

Per-participant, observation-by-observation fitting is now a central analysis
that appears in the main text. Figure~3 shows the full root-mean-squared error
(RMSE) distribution across
participants for every model on every task, with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests against DPE marked on the figure. Table~S1 reports the median
per-participant effect size for every pairwise comparison, Table~S2 the number
of participants for whom each model achieves the lowest RMSE, and Figure~S1
replicates the entire comparison under negative log-likelihood, yielding the
same pattern of wins, ties, and losses.

To review these results: DPE fits significantly better than all three baselines
on Proportion Inference and Value Integration, where it is the best-fitting
model for 86\% and 76\% of participants. On Binary and Continuous Integration it
still beats Mean and LeakyIntegrator (LI), but PR matches it on
the former and outperforms it on the latter, and is the modal best-fitting model
on both (67\% and 70\%). The effect sizes put these on a common scale
(Table~S1): DPE's margin over the other models is 2.3--74.8\% across the
fourteen comparisons it wins (median 14.2\%), whereas PR edges it out by only
0.4\% (not significant) and 2.4\%. PR's advantage is also confined to those two
tasks: elsewhere it fits 30.0\% and 9.4\% worse than DPE and is best for no
participant at all.

Our spiking network fits worse than DPE everywhere, as expected given spike
noise and real-time dynamics, but by very different amounts: second-best of five
on Proportion Inference and Value Integration (5.6\% and 4.9\% behind), fourth
and fifth on Binary and Continuous (30.7\% and 27.9\%). The revised manuscript
drops any claim that DPE is the best available fit to these data in a pure
goodness-of-fit sense, and we state these findings plainly in the Results.

\subsection*{G4. Models compared on neural plausibility}

Dynamic weighting of past evidence has a long history, and the manuscript no
longer claims novelty on that basis. It situates DPE within that family instead:
such models differ principally in how the weight on the newest observation,
$\alpha(n)$, is computed --- held constant, as in the LeakyIntegrator
\citep{usher2001time} and TD(0) \citep{sutton2018reinforcement}; computed online
from surprise or an evolving volatility estimate, as in adaptive-learning-rate
and Kalman-filter-style models \citep{behrens2007learning}; or adjusted
asymmetrically to capture effects such as confirmation bias
\citep{palminteri2017confirmation}. DPE occupies a
deliberately minimal position: $\alpha(n)$ changes with every observation, but
only as a deterministic function of the observation count and a single fitted
exponent.

Instead, we argue that DPE is one member of that family that is simple enough to
actually be computed by a brain in real time, while still being flexible enough
to capture the behavioral signatures discussed above and extensible to more
complicated tasks. The revised Discussion compares models according to this
neural plausibility, and argues that each of the alternative models fails at
least one of the following requirements.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Memory.} DPE carries a single running estimate forward, so its
storage does not grow with the sequence. PR must retain every past observation,
since each observation's weight is recomputed across the entire raw history
whenever a new one arrives. Experience-Weighted Attraction (EWA) maintains a
separate attraction and foregone
payoff for every strategy in a discrete choice set
\citep{camerer1999experience, ho2007selftuning}. Exact Bayesian inference about
a changing environment requires a full posterior over every possible changepoint
location \citep{adams2007bayesian}, which is an unbounded representation that
motivated the development of more tractable delta-rule approximations
\citep{wilson2013mixture}.

\item \textbf{Operations.} DPE's update is a single weighted prediction error.
The alternatives require, respectively, a weighted summation over the full
observation history; a parallel computation across alternatives to select a
response; and updating a posterior whose dimensionality grows with time.

\item \textbf{Origin of bias.} DPE's update implements the optimal running
average at $\lambda=1$; primacy and recency arise as departures from that
optimum when the weight decay is imprecise, which is a reasonable computational
constraint for a biological system. In contrast, PR's two parameters exist so
that the fitted curve can bend into whatever primacy or recency shape the data
display, so its temporal bias is supplied to the model rather than produced by
it.

\end{itemize}

To our knowledge, none of PR, EWA, or exact Bayesian tracking has been realized
in a neural circuit, and we suggest this is not incidental: the features that
make these models flexible are the same ones that make them hard to build from
neurons.

\subsection*{G5. The SNN compared to recurrent neural networks}

The Discussion now contains a dedicated section explaining how the SNN differs
from the recurrent neural networks (RNNs) typically fit to behavioral data:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Its architecture is anatomically interpretable}, comprising
functionally distinct populations whose predictions can be compared to
recordings from distinct cortical areas, rather than an undifferentiated hidden
layer requiring post-hoc decomposition.

\item \textbf{Its single-neuron dynamics are biologically plausible.} Gated
recurrent units commonly fit to choice data \citep{jian2025discovering} rely on
units performing several nonlinear gating operations at once, a level of
complexity implausible for a single biological neuron. We instead use leaky
integrate-and-fire neurons, widely regarded as a reasonable compromise between
biological realism and computational tractability
\citep{eliasmith2013build, gerstner2014neuronal, dayan2005theoretical}.

\item \textbf{Its structure is compositional}, so additional populations can
implement richer rules for $\alpha(n)$ without redesign
\citep{choo2018spaun, dumont2023biologically}, where an RNN is typically
retrained from scratch.

\item \textbf{Its noise is emergent rather than fitted}, following unavoidably
from representing a continuous value in a finite spiking population, whereas an
RNN's noise is injected or fit as a free parameter. This property produces the
variability result in G2.

\item \textbf{It is validated against real human behavior}, whereas the closest
neural models have only been tested on synthetic tracking or regression tasks
\citep{millidge2021neural, adamiat2025spiking}.

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{G6. Four new predictions relating neural activity to behavior}

The Discussion now contains a dedicated section stating four predictions made by our SNN,
each with the mechanism that generates it and a specific proposed measurement:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Following a surprising observation, the peak transient prediction-error
response predicts how quickly that response decays back to baseline. Both effects are explained 
by how the error response scales with the baseline learning rate $\alpha_0$ (Fig.~6A--C).

\item The within-trial decline in error-selective firing predicts the decline in the magnitude of
response updates. Both effects are explained by how error neuron activity scales
with the discounting rate $\lambda$ during trials with many observations (Fig.~6D--F).

\item The signal-to-noise ratio of the decoded error signal predicts behavioral
response variability $\sigma$ (Fig.~6G--I). Both effects are explained by limited reprsentational capacity
in the error population, a result that we corroborated by a decoder-free
split-half spike-count measure (Fig.~S4).

\item Perturbation during the inter-trial interval (ITI), which mimics a distractor task or
non-invasive stimulation delivered during a working-memory delay, increases response error and
variability in a dose-dependent way. However, this only occurs in networks
that rely on activity-dependent working memory;

\rc{1.1 Novelty, and whether the network advances the state of the
art}{I do question the novelty of the approach. Reinforcement learning is
obviously not new and neither is the notion of learning rates that decrease over
time\ldots{} the Yoo et al.\ (2025) article does describe how their ADM ``can
represent a process in which subjects maintain their current estimate of AE and

We agree with the premise, and the revised manuscript no longer claims
otherwise. It situates DPE within the family of dynamic-weighting models and
compares those models on whether their weighting of past evidence could be
computed by a brain in real time, rather than on the novelty of the weighting
scheme itself (G4). We also agree that the ADM was underweighted in the original submission, and
have addressed that by fitting its temporal weighting scheme as a baseline model
on all four tasks (renamed PrimacyRecency, PR), where it is a strong competitor (G3). We
should be explicit about what that baseline is: our PR model implements
\citet{yoo2025people}'s Eq.~1 exactly,
\[
	w(o,n) = \left[1 - \left(1-\varepsilon_p^{\,o}\right)
	\left(1-\varepsilon_r^{\,n-o+1}\right)\right](1-\eta) + \eta,
\]
with $\eta$ fixed at 0.01, together with the weighted average over stored
observations that forms the ADM's decision variable. We omit only the diffusion
process, which our continuously reported estimates do not require, and we name
the model for the weighting scheme it implements rather than for the diffusion

This bears on the passage the reviewer quotes. \citet{yoo2025people} note that

observation history, which is the memory cost set out in G4.

On whether the network advances on existing neural implementations, we set out

\rc{1.4 The ``context'' population}{\ldots{}say more about the purpose of the
``context'' population\ldots{} The label ``context'' is potentially confusing
because it implies that it represents something about the choice environment.}

In the revised manuscript, our default (recurrent) implementation contains no
such population. It comprises three functional components: \textit{error}, which
is two-dimensional and represents the discounting weight $\alpha(n)$ alongside
the raw prediction error $I_n - V_{n-1}$; \textit{value}, which holds the
running estimate and maintains it between observations through a recurrent
connection; and the counting network \textit{count}, which tracks the
observation index and decodes $\alpha(n)$. A single connection from
\textit{error} to \textit{value} computes the product of \textit{error}'s two
represented dimensions, which is the weighted prediction error that drives each
update.
% No baseline population is required to represent signed prediction
% errors, since a Neural Engineering Framework (NEF) population represents
% positive and negative values alike.

The synaptic implementation does use such a population, and here we agree the
original label was misleading; we have renamed it \textit{background}. It fires
at a constant, non-selective rate and carries no information about the
observation or the running estimate. Its role is to supply presynaptic activity
for the plastic connection onto \textit{value}: its spikes enter the Prescribed
Error Sensitivity learning rule (Eq.~8) as the presynaptic activity term $a_i$,
which the decoded error signal multiplies against when the weights are updated.
Precisely because that spiking carries no information, the entire estimate ends
up stored in the weights rather than in ongoing activity --- the
``activity-silent'' mechanism this implementation exists to test. Materials and
Methods now sets out both implementations in full.

\rc{1.5 Synaptic versus recurrent predictions, and ITI length}{I was wondering
whether the models make different predictions about how shorter or longer ITI's
might affect learning?}

This comment prompted what is now one of the central results of the paper, and
we thank the reviewer for it. We addressed the underlying question --- whether
the two mechanisms differ in their dependence on ongoing neural activity during
the delay --- with a causal perturbation during the ITI rather than a variation
in its length (G6, prediction~4). Across perturbation strengths, the recurrent
model's error and response variability both grow reliably ($r$~=~0.77 and
$r$~=~0.58 respectively, both $p<0.0001$), while the synaptic model is
unaffected (both $r \approx 0$, $p>0.9$; Fig.~7C--D).
We chose this design over an ITI-duration manipulation for three reasons. First, it
yields a graded dose--response relationship and a sharper test of
the dissociation. Second, it maps directly onto a feasible human experiment: a
distractor task or transcranial stimulation applied during the delay. Third, ITI
duration is confounded with total session length and participant fatigue; during
informal testing we saw no difference between a short and a long ITI, which
suggested the manipulation would add substantial time to an already long session
for limited sensitivity. We would be glad to add an ITI-duration analysis if the
reviewer still considers it necessary.

\rc{2.1 Relationship to Experience-Weighted Attraction}{\ldots{}the idea of
nesting strategies by weighted updates is not new\ldots{} The proposed
formulation appears closely related to the Experience-Weighted Attraction model
by Colin Camerer and colleagues\ldots{} The EWA model has also been widely
applied and empirically tested across behavioral and neuroscience contexts. In
this light, the current model seems to constitute a simplified variant of an
already well-established approach.}

We take this point seriously, and it prompted a substantial revision of the
relevant Discussion section. We do not dispute that EWA is a more general and
more flexible framework for weighted updating, nor that dynamic weighting of
past evidence has a long history; the manuscript no longer claims novelty on
that basis, and situates DPE within that family instead (G4). We instead make
the narrower claims that DPE captures human behavior and individual differences
across four heterogeneous tasks and can be realized in a biologically plausible
spiking circuit, unlike EWA and the other alternatives we discuss (G4).

Regarding the applicability of EWA to evidence integration, EWA was developed
for repeated strategic interaction, where a player faces other agents over a
defined strategy space and can weigh the foregone payoff of strategies not
chosen. That setting is what motivates its machinery, including the
per-strategy bookkeeping described in G4. Our tasks include neither choices nor
foregone payoffs: participants report a continuous estimate of a quantity, with
no alternatives to compare and nothing counterfactual to evaluate. So far as we
are aware, EWA has not been applied to non-social sequential estimation of the
sort studied here, nor tested outside strategic games. Applying it here would
require substantial reformulation (e.g., defining a strategy set and a payoff
function where our tasks have neither) and it is not clear that the
flexibility and empirical coverage the reviewer describes would survive that
reformulation, since the machinery responsible for them has no counterpart in a
task with a single continuous response. Such an exploration is beyond the scope
of this study. Nonetheless, we have added citations to the EWA literature,
including the self-tuning variant
\citep{camerer1999experience, ho2007selftuning}.

\rc{2.2 New insights, observations, structures, constraints, and
predictions}{\ldots{}it remains unclear whether the proposed model yields
genuinely new insights into the mechanisms of information
aggregation\ldots{} Beyond demonstrating that the model can account for
individual variability, it provides limited evidence of new observations,
structures, constraints, or testable predictions.}

This comment drove the largest additions to the manuscript, and we answer it in
the reviewer's own terms.

\textbf{New observations (G2).} These include the within-trial decay of response
change, reliable at the level of individual participants rather than only in the
aggregate; the growth and autocorrelation of response variability under exact
sequence repetition; and the cross-task stability of both discounting rate and
response precision.

\textbf{New constraints.} First, the SNN's representational precision is
limited by the finite, heterogeneous population of spiking neurons that encodes
each quantity. One consequence of this
constraint is that the SNN reproduces both variability signatures (Fig.~5D--I)
without fitting dedicated noise parameters, whereas every
response-noise-augmented mathematical model we tested fails to reproduce them.
This implies that response-stage noise is insufficient, and that the noise must
reside in a persistent, evolving state representation (G2).
\citet{prat2024imprecise} reached a similar conclusion by comparing
mathematical models with and without compounding state noise against behavioral
data, but did not specify what mechanism produces that noise; our contribution
is to ground state noise in a neural process and show that it generalizes across tasks.
Second, DPE's power-law decay is a strong constraint on the form of dynamic PE
weighting, and reflects constraints on how such functions are computed by
the brain in real time (G4). The alternatives apply different constraints: PR must retain
the full observation history and recompute every weight as each observation
arrives; EWA requires per-strategy bookkeeping over a choice set; and exact
Bayesian tracking requires an unbounded posterior. Third, our implementation is
constrained in ways RNNs are not: leaky integrate-and-fire neurons,
anatomically distinct populations, and emergent representaitonal noise (G5).

\textbf{New structures.} We implement DPE in two biologically distinct circuits
--- one storing the running estimate in persistent recurrent activity, the other
in plastic synaptic weights updated by an error-driven learning rule. They fit
human behavior comparably but are causally dissociable by perturbation during
the inter-trial interval (G6, prediction~4).

\textbf{New testable predictions.} We list four new predictions, alongside the
mechanisms that generate them and proposed empirical measurements to validate them (G6).

\rc{2.3 Reduced-form measures of behavior}{The use of a reduced-form measure for
behavior in relatively complex tasks results in oversimplifying the findings.}

This describes a real problem with how the original submission was organized:
the trial-by-trial model comparison was present, but it sat in supplementary
material while the main text led with summary measures drawn from the source
studies. In the revised manuscript, per-participant, observation-by-observation
fits are a central analysis that appears immediately in the Results, with effect
sizes and significance for every comparison; the behavioral signatures we report
alongside them are likewise computed per participant rather than only in the
aggregate (G2, G3). We would add, though, that those aggregate measures are doing different work
from summary statistics. Response change and response variability under
repetition are dynamical signatures of the integration process, and they are
informative precisely because the fitting procedure does not target them. A
model fit to minimize error can match a participant's responses closely while
failing to reproduce qualitative features of the underlying process ---
perseveration \citep{katahira2018statistical}, confirmation bias
\citep{palminteri2017confirmation, palminteri2022computational}, or sequential
choice-history effects \citep{yu2008sequential, zhang2014sequential}. We make
this argument explicitly in the Results and again in the Discussion, following
prior work urging exactly this kind of complementary test
\citep{palminteri2017falsification, wilson2019ten}.

\rc{2.4 ``Neural correlates'' terminology}{\ldots{}the paper frequently refers to
``neural correlates'' when discussing SNN activity, which could be
misleading\ldots{} There is a significant inferential leap between a SNN model
and the actual biological implementation in humans.}

We agree entirely, and have removed this framing. The phrase no longer appears
anywhere in the manuscript, and we no longer claim that the model reproduces
neural data. Every statement relating network activity to the brain is now
framed as a prediction, with the measurement that would test it stated
explicitly (G6). The Discussion now closes with a Limitations section stating
plainly that no spiking, imaging, or perturbation recordings exist for these
tasks, so each of our neural predictions remains prospective.

\section*{The social-learning task and fMRI comparison have been removed}

We removed the social learning task for a methodological
reason. The revised paper emphasizes two behavioral signatures of the integration
process: the magnitude of response change between consecutive observations
($\Delta R$), and the variability of responses to an exactly repeated sequence
($\sigma$). Neither is defined for the social-learning task, which collected a
binary choice rather than a continuous estimate, and involved non-repeatable social interaction.
Unfortunately, removing the task also removed the associated fMRI comparison \citep{jiang2023neurocomputational}.
We believe the replacement is an improvement: rather than a post-hoc comparison to one
published result, the manuscript now makes four quantitative neural-behavioral
predictions (G6), specifies the measurements needed to validate them,
and states the prospective nature of these predictions plainly in the Limitations section. 
We would rather make falsifiable claims about
neural activity than draw a high-level correspondence to measurements collected
under tasks outside our core paradigm.
