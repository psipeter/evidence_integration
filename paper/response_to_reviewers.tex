We thank both reviewers for their careful and constructive evaluations of our original submission. Their comments identified three gaps in that manuscript: (C1) our reporting of model fit quality was neither sufficiently transparent nor sufficiently central; (C2) our neural analyses stopped short of the quantitative, testable claims a network model should support; and (C3) the case for our neural network as an advance over existing mathematical and recurrent neural network models was underdeveloped. Additionally, the editor requested that a revised manuscript include (C4) additional experiments. In response, we substantially rewrote the manuscript to emphasize fit quality and our advances over existing models, added four simulated neural experiments that generate falsifiable predictions, designed and collected two new behavioral experiments, and analyzed additional behavioral signatures of individual variability. We first summarize how these changes address the reviewers' core concerns, then address each comment individually in greater detail, referencing new text and figures in the manuscript directly.

\section*{General concerns}

\subsection*{C1. Reporting of model fit quality was not transparent or central}

Both reviewers asked for a more transparent account of how well each model
actually fits the data, and we agree that the original submission fell short.
The trial-by-trial model comparison was present, but it sat in supplementary
material while the main text led with summary measures drawn from the source
studies. We have changed how model performance is reported throughout the
manuscript. Per-participant, observation-by-observation fitting is now a
central analysis that appears immediately in the Results; we no longer claim
that DPE is the best available fit to these data in a pure goodness-of-fit
sense; and where it loses, we say so and give the number. We replicated the
entire comparison under a second fit metric, negative log-likelihood, using
each model's response-noise variant so that a likelihood is well defined, and
the pattern of wins, ties, and losses is unchanged
(Fig.~\ref{fig:model_performance_nll}). We do also argue that aggregate fit
quality should not be the only criterion deciding between these models, since
it does not capture the dynamical signatures that distinguish their mechanisms;
we set that argument out under C3.

\textbf{Results: \textit{Comparing performance across models and tasks}}

We assessed how well each model accounts for human behavior by fitting its free parameters separately to every participant and computing the root-mean-squared error (RMSE) between each model's predicted responses and the participant's actual responses. We compared DPE and its SNN implementation against three baseline models spanning a range of integration strategies: Mean, which computes the optimal running average with no free parameters \citep{degroot1974reaching}; LeakyIntegrator (LI), which uses the same error-driven update but with a constant learning rate \citep{usher2001time, glaze2015normative}; and PrimacyRecency (PR), which up-weights early and late observations via two independently fit decay parameters \citep{pooley2011understanding, galdo2022quest, yoo2025people}. Model equations and fitting procedures are detailed in Materials and Methods, and were unchanged across tasks; only free parameters were allowed to vary.

DPE achieved the best, or among the best, fit to human behavior on every task (Fig.~\ref{fig:model_performance}). It had significantly lower RMSE than all three baseline models on both Proportion Inference and Value Integration, where it was also the single best-fitting model for the large majority of participants (86\% and 76\%, respectively). On Binary and Continuous Integration, DPE significantly outperformed Mean and LI, but PR matched it on the former and significantly outperformed it on the latter, making PR the modal best-fitting individual model on both (67\% and 70\%, respectively; see Table~\ref{tab:best_fit}). However, these per-participant win counts overstate the size of this gap: the median RMSE difference between DPE and PR was negligible on Binary Integration (0.4\%, not significant, $p$=0.40) and modest even where reliable on Continuous Integration (2.4\% lower for PR, $p$=0.004), with negative log-likelihood showing the same pattern (see Supplementary Text). Table~\ref{tab:effect_sizes} places this in context: DPE's median margin over the other models across all four tasks is 14.2\% (range 2.3--74.8\%), substantially larger than the 0.4\% and 2.4\% by which PR edges it out. PR's advantage was also confined to these two tasks: on Proportion Inference and Value Integration it fit 30.0\% and 9.4\% worse than DPE, and was the best-fitting model for no participant at all. The SNN fit worst of all five models on Continuous Integration and worse than two of the three baselines on Binary Integration, but remained close to the baselines on Proportion Inference (+5.6\%) and Value Integration (+4.9\%), reflecting the added cost of spike noise and real-time dynamics.
\end{ms}

\subsection*{C2. No new predictions from the neural network model}

The Discussion now contains a dedicated section stating four predictions made by our SNN,
each with the mechanism that generates it and a specific proposed measurement:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Following a surprising observation, the peak transient prediction-error
response predicts how quickly that response decays back to baseline. Both effects are explained 
by how the error response scales with the baseline learning rate $\alpha_0$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}A--C).

\item The within-trial decline in error-selective firing predicts the decline in the magnitude of
response updates. Both effects are explained by how error neuron activity scales
with the discounting rate $\lambda$ during trials with many observations (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}D--F).

\item The signal-to-noise ratio of the decoded error signal predicts behavioral
response variability $\sigma$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:neural_main}G--I). Both effects are explained by limited representational capacity
in the error population, a result that we corroborated by a decoder-free
split-half spike-count measure (Fig.~\ref{fig:decoder_free}).

\item Perturbation during the inter-trial interval (ITI), which mimics a distractor task or
non-invasive stimulation delivered during a working-memory delay, increases response error and
variability in a dose-dependent way. However, this only occurs in networks
that rely on activity-dependent working memory;
% ($r_{error}$~=~0.77, $r_{\sigma}$~=~0.58, both $p<0.0001$)
networks that rely on synaptic plasticity quickly recover from such perturbation
(Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}C--D).
%  (both $r \approx 0$, $p>0.9$, Fig.~\ref{fig:synaptic_main}C--D).
% This effect is explained by
% how noise interacts with the neural mechanisms of working memory in
% each network implementation: the WM model needs continuous recurrent feedback
% to hold its estimate, which is disrupted directly by the perturbation;
% whereas the synaptic model only changes its stored value in response to a
% sustained, non-zero-mean error signal, which unstructured
% noise does not produce.
This asymmetry reflects each
mechanism's dependence on ongoing activity: the activity-dependent network needs
continuous recurrent feedback to hold its estimate,
which is disrupted directly by the perturbation;
whereas the synaptic plasticity network only changes its stored value
in response to a sustained, non-zero-mean error signal, which unstructured
noise does not produce.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{C3. The case for our neural network as an advance over existing mathematical and recurrent neural network models was underdeveloped}

Dynamic weighting of past evidence has a long history, and the manuscript no
longer claims novelty on that basis. It situates DPE within that family instead:
such models differ principally in how the weight on the newest observation,
$\alpha(n)$, is computed --- held constant, as in the LeakyIntegrator
\citep{usher2001time} and TD(0) \citep{sutton2018reinforcement}; computed online
from surprise or an evolving volatility estimate, as in adaptive-learning-rate
and Kalman-filter-style models \citep{behrens2007learning}; or adjusted
asymmetrically to capture effects such as confirmation bias
\citep{palminteri2017confirmation}. DPE occupies a
deliberately minimal position: $\alpha(n)$ changes with every observation, but
only as a deterministic function of the observation count and a single fitted
exponent.

Instead, we argue that DPE is one member of that family that is simple enough to
actually be computed by a brain in real time, while still being flexible enough
to capture the behavioral signatures discussed above and extensible to more
complicated tasks. The revised Discussion compares models according to this
neural plausibility, and argues that each of the alternative models fails at
least one of the following requirements.

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Memory.} DPE carries a single running estimate forward, so its
storage does not grow with the sequence. PR must retain every past observation,
since each observation's weight is recomputed across the entire raw history
whenever a new one arrives. Experience-Weighted Attraction (EWA) maintains a
separate attraction and foregone
payoff for every strategy in a discrete choice set
\citep{camerer1999experience, ho2007selftuning}. Exact Bayesian inference about
a changing environment requires a full posterior over every possible changepoint
location \citep{adams2007bayesian}, which is an unbounded representation that
motivated the development of more tractable delta-rule approximations
\citep{wilson2013mixture}.

\item \textbf{Operations.} DPE's update is a single weighted prediction error.
The alternatives require, respectively, a weighted summation over the full
observation history; a parallel computation across alternatives to select a
response; and updating a posterior whose dimensionality grows with time.

\item \textbf{Origin of bias.} DPE's update implements the optimal running
average at $\lambda=1$; primacy and recency arise as departures from that
optimum when the weight decay is imprecise, which is a reasonable computational
constraint for a biological system. In contrast, PR's two parameters exist so
that the fitted curve can bend into whatever primacy or recency shape the data
display, so its temporal bias is supplied to the model rather than produced by
it.

\end{itemize}

To our knowledge, none of PR, EWA, or exact Bayesian tracking has been realized
in a neural circuit, and we suggest this is not incidental: the features that
make these models flexible are the same ones that make them hard to build from
neurons.

Furthermore, the Discussion now contains a dedicated section explaining how the SNN differs
from the recurrent neural networks (RNNs) typically fit to behavioral data:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{Its architecture is anatomically interpretable}, comprising
functionally distinct populations whose predictions can be compared to
recordings from distinct cortical areas, rather than an undifferentiated hidden
layer requiring post-hoc decomposition.

\item \textbf{Its single-neuron dynamics are biologically plausible.} Gated
recurrent units commonly fit to choice data \citep{jian2025discovering} rely on
units performing several nonlinear gating operations at once, a level of
complexity implausible for a single biological neuron. We instead use leaky
integrate-and-fire neurons, widely regarded as a reasonable compromise between
biological realism and computational tractability
\citep{eliasmith2013build, gerstner2014neuronal, dayan2005theoretical}.

\item \textbf{Its structure is compositional}, so additional populations can
implement richer rules for $\alpha(n)$ without redesign
\citep{choo2018spaun, dumont2023biologically}, where an RNN is typically
retrained from scratch.

\item \textbf{Its noise is emergent rather than fitted}, following unavoidably
from representing a continuous value in a finite spiking population, whereas an
RNN's noise is injected or fit as a free parameter. This property produces the
variability result in C4.

\item \textbf{It is validated against real human behavior}, whereas the closest
neural models have only been tested on synthetic tracking or regression tasks
\citep{millidge2021neural, adamiat2025spiking}.

\end{itemize}

\subsection*{C4. Request for additional experiments}

We designed and collected two new tasks, Binary Integration and Continuous
Integration, completed by the same 46 participants. Each supplies something the
existing tasks did not: a within-subject design that tests whether individual
differences are stable traits rather than task-specific artifacts; trials long
enough (15 observations) to estimate a discounting rate reliably; and
deliberately repeated sequences, which make response variability measurable. The
manuscript now spans four tasks (Fig.~\ref{fig:tasks_overview}), which vary in input type, sequence
length, repetition structure, and estimated quantity while sharing one response
format: a continuous, real-valued estimate reported after every observation.

As a direct result of analyzing these experiments, we report three new behavioral findings:

\textbf{Response change declines within a trial.} The magnitude of response
change ($\Delta R$) declines within a trial, and does so reliably at the level of individual participants rather than only in
the aggregate: a per-participant rank correlation between $\Delta R$ and
observation number is significantly negative for 85\%, 59\%, and 68\% of
participants in Binary, Continuous, and Value Integration, and a fitted
power-law discounting rate $\lambda$ differs significantly from zero for 98\%,
77\%, and 72\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_main}). This decline is the behavioral signature of a learning
rate that decays as evidence accumulates; only models with the associated
mechanisms --- Discounted Prediction Error (DPE), PrimacyRecency (PR), and our
spiking neural network (SNN) ---
can reproduce individual variability in this signature.

\textbf{Response variability grows and persists under repetition.} Human
responses to an exactly repeated sequence grow more variable as the sequence
progresses (1.3--1.9$\times$ from first to last observation), and
response residuals are positively autocorrelated across consecutive responses
($r$~=~0.40--0.62 at lag~1; Fig.~\ref{fig:sigma_main}D--I). Only the SNN
reproduces these signatures (2.0--3.2$\times$; $r$~=~0.69--0.76), and it does so
without any fitted noise parameter; every alternative model we tested captures
neither (0.93--1.15$\times$; autocorrelation $\approx$~0) despite each being
given one fitted response noise parameter. This indicates that noise must reside
in a persistent, evolving state representation rather than arising during the response period.

\textbf{Discounting rate and response precision are stable individual traits.}
An individual's discounting rate ($\lambda$) and response precision ($\sigma$)
are both reliable within task (split-half $r$~=~0.77--0.92 and
$r$~=~0.62--0.95 respectively) and transfer across tasks ($\sigma$: $r$~=~0.70,
$p<10^{-7}$; $\lambda$: $r$~=~0.31, $p$~=~0.037; Fig.~\ref{fig:lambda_sigma_reliability}), indicating that they
reflect stable individual traits. The weaker cross-task correlation of $\lambda$
suggests that this property may also reflect task-specific strategy.

The synaptic implementation does use such a population, and here we agree the

\textbf{New observations (C4).} These include the within-trial decay of response

\section*{The social-learning task and fMRI comparison have been removed}

We removed the social learning task for a methodological
reason. The revised paper emphasizes two behavioral signatures of the integration
process: the magnitude of response change between consecutive observations
($\Delta R$), and the variability of responses to an exactly repeated sequence
($\sigma$). Neither is defined for the social-learning task, which collected a
binary choice rather than a continuous estimate, and involved non-repeatable
social interaction. The task also sat awkwardly with the rest of the model
suite: because evidence arrived in discrete social-exchange stages rather than
as a stream of comparable observations, fitting it required an ad-hoc component
with a separate free weight for each stage, which has no counterpart in the
other three tasks and no principled analogue in DPE's single discounting
exponent.
Unfortunately, removing the task also removed the associated fMRI comparison \citep{jiang2023neurocomputational}.
We believe the replacement is an improvement: rather than a post-hoc comparison to one
published result, the manuscript now makes four quantitative neural-behavioral
predictions (C2), specifies the measurements needed to validate them,
and states the prospective nature of these predictions plainly in the Limitations section. 
We would rather make falsifiable claims at the level our model
actually operates at --- spiking activity in identified populations, which is
what the network produces --- than draw a high-level correspondence between
the model and BOLD measurements collected under a task outside our core
paradigm.
